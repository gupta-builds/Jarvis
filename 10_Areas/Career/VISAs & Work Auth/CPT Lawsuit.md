# CPT Lawsuit
Four higher-education groups sued the federal government on October 5, 2026 over the August CPT messages, and the first court date is November 6. This note records the case itself: who is suing, what each count argues, the 35-year history the plaintiffs rely on, and what the docket shows so far. Read it before the hearing, again after the government's response, and once more after any order.

## The Parties
==The plaintiffs are associations suing for their members, and the University of Minnesota, Twin Cities is on AAU's published member list, so a ruling covering AAU's members would plausibly reach my school.== The AAU describes itself in the complaint as 71 research universities in the United States and Canada, 69 of them American. The Presidents' Alliance represents close to 600 colleges in forty-two states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. AICUM represents 55 private colleges in Massachusetts. NAFSA has more than 9,000 members at more than 2,300 institutions, including the DSOs themselves ([[SEVP and DSOs]]).
The defendants include the Department of Justice and the Attorney General because the messages warn of penalties for "providing false information," and perjury is prosecuted by DOJ. The scope of any order is not decided: the plaintiffs ask for relief as to themselves, their members, and the members' employees and students, so whether students at non-member schools benefit depends on what the judge grants.
## The Four Counts
Every count arises under the Administrative Procedure Act, which lets a court set aside agency action that is arbitrary, contrary to law, or issued without required procedure.
1. **Arbitrary and capricious**
	An agency changing position must show it knows it is changing, explain why, and weigh the reliance of people who built plans on the old position. The plaintiffs say the messages "do not even acknowledge" they announce a new reading, call themselves "reminders," and ignore institutions' reliance, the cost to students, the lack of a workable alternative, and the educational reasons programs keep training optional.
2. **Unlawfully retroactive**
	Neither message says it applies only going forward, and both threaten consequences for approvals already made. The plaintiffs allege DHS officers have begun asking students about earlier CPT when they apply for other benefits, which would treat past, lawful approvals under the new test.
3. **Contrary to law**
	An agency is bound by its own regulation and cannot amend it through guidance. The plaintiffs say the regulation's text, structure and history conflict with the messages.
4. **Procedurally invalid**
	The messages set binding eligibility standards backed by enforcement threats, which makes them legislative rules that needed notice and comment. DHS published no proposal, took no comments, and claimed no good-cause exception.
==The case turns on one reading: whether "integral part of an established curriculum" means training that earns academic credit, or only training required of everyone.==
## The History Behind The Plaintiffs' Reading
The complaint's regulatory history is the most useful part for understanding why "this was always the rule" is contested.
1. **1947 and 1953**
	The first practical-training regulations allowed work "required or recommended by the school," so training was never limited to degree requirements.
2. **1964 and 1978**
	Students at schools with alternate work-study courses as part of the regular curriculum could take them without a separate work application, with the employment counted as practical training.
3. **1987**
	The term "curricular practical training" first appears. Guidance said an "elective internship that is not required for the attainment of the degree or diploma is considered curricular practical training."
4. **1991**
	INS proposed a rule defining integral training as training "for which students are awarded academic credits." Commenters from hotel management, nursing, law, engineering and teaching asked for required non-credit internships to count too, and the final rule added "required internship or practicum." The final rule also said a student could do elective curricular training "after having been in status for nine months." That is the two-pathway reading: credit, or required if no credit.
5. **1992**
	INS cables said for-credit training "may be mandatory or elective" and that non-credit training must be required.
6. **2013 to 2022**
	An October 2013 SEVP newsletter said CPT "must be part of a student's program of study, either required for completion or optional, but for academic credit." SEVP's DSO training, available from at least 2014 to 2022, said it must be required or the student must receive credit.
7. **March 2025**
	Study in the States still described CPT as training that must be an integral part of the academic program and directly related to the major.
The plaintiffs add a textual argument. The same 1991 rule lets on-campus work happen at an educationally affiliated off-campus location if it is "an integral part of the student's educational program," meant to cover graduate research assistants on professors' grants, which no degree universally requires. "Integral" therefore cannot mean mandatory.
## The Harm Argument
The memorandum rests irreparable harm on four points: lost, time-limited training that cannot be made up (especially for students in their final year), the threat of losing SEVP certification, compliance costs already being spent, and damage to institutions' standing with prospective students. More than 130,000 students were authorized for CPT in 2024, according to ICE's own report that the complaint cites.
The complaint also answers the obvious fallback of pre-completion OPT. It is not a substitute because the student needs school approval plus a USCIS permit, cannot apply until 90 days before the start, carries a full course load, is capped at 20 hours in term, and burns OPT time. As of September 28, USCIS's own case-processing page showed only 80 percent of F-1 student OPT applications finished within five months, the complaint says ([[Pre-Completion OPT]]).

## What Each Outcome Would Mean
This section is analysis, not source text.
- *Stay or injunction granted:* the August messages are postponed or barred for the covered plaintiffs and their members. A school like UMN could return to the credit-or-required reading, but ISSS would still need to rebuild its process. The government can appeal, and in the duration of status case it filed its appeal 16 days after losing ([[Duration of Status Rule]]).
- *Denied:* the guidance stays in force while the case continues, and schools remain where they are.
- *Partial:* relief limited to plaintiffs' members would leave students elsewhere unprotected.
- *No decision soon:* the docket shows no deadline for a ruling, so planning for summer 2027 on a hoped-for win is a gamble ([[Junior Year Internship Decision]]).
