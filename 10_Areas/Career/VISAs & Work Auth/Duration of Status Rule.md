# Duration of Status Rule
In July 2026 DHS finalized a rule that would have ended the open-ended admission F-1 students have had since 1978 and replaced it with a fixed date. A federal court postponed it the day before it took effect, and on November 16 the same judge cancelled it outright. The government can still appeal, so it is the rule most likely to rearrange the dates in my own timeline if it comes back. This note captures what the rule says, why DHS wrote it, what was argued in court, what the judge held, what happens next, and which pages are wrong about it. Read it whenever a page quotes a 30-day window.
## In Plain Words
Today an F-1 student is admitted with no end date. I can stay as long as I keep studying or working on OPT. The July 2026 rule would have given every student an end date, the "Admit Until Date," of up to four years, and anyone who needs more time, including time for OPT, would have to apply to extend it. On November 16 Judge Saylor vacated the rule, which means he cancelled it for good in the trial court. It is no longer paused, it is void, and the old system with the open-ended I-94 and the 60-day windows is the law.
The catch is that this is the end of the first round, not the end of the case. The government can appeal, and a higher court could bring the rule back. As of November 18 I found no appeal of the final ruling. The ISSS advisor's October line, "there's still an injunction, but we don't know if or when that would be lifted," is out of date, because a final judgment replaced the injunction ([[ISSS October Meeting Brief]], [[US Immigration Rules Explained]]).
For me the worry is paperwork and timing. If the rule ever returns, OPT windows would shrink from 60 to 30 days, and I would probably file an extension application together with my OPT application ([[2028 Graduation Timeline]]).

## What Duration Of Status Was
==Duration of status meant an F-1 student was admitted with no end date and could stay for as long as they kept studying or doing authorized practical training, plus a grace period.== The I-94 read "D/S." The fixed-period rule changes that so every F admission carries an Admit Until Date, and anyone needing more time must ask for it.
DHS's reasons are in the rule's executive summary. F admissions were over 1.8 million in fiscal 2024, up more than 11 percent from 2023 and far above roughly 260,000 in the 1980-81 school year. DHS says D/S does not give officers enough chances to verify that students are doing only what the status allows, it found over 2,100 people who first entered as F-1 students between 2000 and 2010 still in active F-1 status as of April 2025, and it points to the post-9/11 push to track students through SEVIS. It concludes that admission for D/S "is not appropriate" and returns F-1 to the practice before 1979 ([[SEVP and DSOs]]).
## What The Rule Changes For F Students
These are the terms of the vacated rule. I keep them here because the rule can return.
1. **A fixed admission period**
	Admission for up to the program length on the I-20, not more than four years, plus 30 days to arrive and 30 days to depart. English language training is limited to 24 months. Public high school is limited to 12 months in total.
2. **Extension of stay**
	A student needing more time to finish, start a new program, or do post-completion OPT or STEM OPT must file Form I-539 with USCIS, with the fee and biometrics, after a DSO recommends it in SEVIS. Alternatively the student can leave and be readmitted by CBP with a new period. DHS recommends applying 180 days before the date expires.
3. **OPT dates**
	During OPT the Admit Until Date is the end date on the work permit plus 30 days to depart.
4. **Departure period**
	30 days after the program or OPT ends, down from 60.
5. **Working during a pending extension**
	A timely filing before the 30-day departure period lets a student keep on-campus work, hardship work, and CPT for up to 240 days. Filing during the departure period means all work stops until approval.
6. **Academic mobility limits**
	Below the graduate level, no change of educational level, major, or school in the first year without an SEVP exception. At the graduate level and above, none at any point in the program. After finishing, no enrolling in a program at the same or a lower level.
7. **Journalists (I visas)**
	Admission capped at 240 days, with a shorter period for some passports from China, and extensions decided without appeal.
## The Transition For Current Students
Also the vacated rule's terms.
- A student in the country under D/S on the effective date may stay without an extension until the program end date or the end of valid OPT or STEM OPT, not exceeding four years, plus 60 days to depart.
- A student already in the country under D/S who files for post-completion OPT or STEM OPT on or before **March 18, 2027** needs no extension of stay. DHS's FAQ calls this "within six months of the effective date," but the rule text fixes the calendar date, and says the date may be extended if DHS decides it is necessary.
- A student who leaves and is readmitted gets a fixed period, and must then file an extension of stay together with the OPT application.
- A student with a timely cap-subject H-1B petition who qualifies for cap-gap needs no extension and stays in F-1 until approval or April 1 ([[Cap-Gap Extension]]).
## Where The Lawsuit Stands
1. **August 18**
	NAFSA, the Presidents' Alliance, AICUM, two teachers' and graduate-employee unions, the UAW and its local, and the NewsGuild-CWA sued in the District of Massachusetts, No. 1:26-cv-13799, before Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV. McDermott Will & Schulte represents them.
2. **September 3 and 14**
	After a hearing on September 3, the judge on September 14 granted relief under section 705 of the Administrative Procedure Act, which postpones the effective date. He found the plaintiffs likely to succeed on arbitrary and capricious, and the docket calls the order a preliminary injunction postponing the rule. His order denied vacatur and summary judgment "without prejudice to its renewal."
3. **September 30 to October 19**
	The government filed notice of appeal on September 30. At an October 2 conference it said it would ask the First Circuit to expedite and wanted district proceedings paused. On October 19 the First Circuit set an expedited schedule with the government's brief due November 9, the plaintiffs' December 9, and the government's reply December 19. The government asked only to expedite and did not seek to lift the order.
4. **October 30 to November 2**
	The Justice Department moved for summary judgment, saying DHS considered nearly 22,000 public comments. The plaintiffs filed a supplemental brief asking the court to vacate the rule. DHS replied on November 2 asking for judgment or, failing that, a remand without vacatur.
5. **November 3 and 13**
	The government asked the First Circuit to hold its appeal for 30 days so the district court could reach a final judgment and everything could be appealed together. The First Circuit granted that on November 13, with the plaintiffs' consent. The hold runs to about December 13.
6. **November 16**
	Judge Saylor granted summary judgment to the plaintiffs, denied the government's cross-motion, and vacated the rule. It is an eight-page order, Dkt. 73. I found no oral argument before it. He decided it on the briefs.
The appeal does not revive the rule. The rule stays void unless a court stays the ruling or reverses it, so the 60-day rules are the operative ones ([[Visa Litigation Tracker]]).
## What Was Argued On September 3
This is the only oral argument I found in the case. It ran from 10:59 a.m. to 12:38 p.m. in Courtroom 1, in person only, so there is no livestream or video, and the official 72-page transcript is the record. Paul Hughes of McDermott Will & Schulte argued for the plaintiffs. Michael Celone and Oleksiy Isaev of the Justice Department argued for DHS. My text conversion of the transcript lost the first minutes of the government's opening, so for that stretch I am relying on the judge's questions. The hearing was a motion for a preliminary injunction or, alternatively, summary judgment.
### The plaintiffs
- **The agency treated a known cost as zero.** DHS admitted the rule could "diminish the economic benefits" students bring, then called the enrollment loss unquantifiable and counted it as nothing. Hughes cited an expert estimate in the comment record that even a 10 percent drop costs tens of billions of dollars a year.
- **SEVIS already does the job.** Schools update SEVIS every term. A school official must report a change, such as a student dropping out, within 21 days, under penalty of perjury, and schools are audited every two years. Hughes said the record shows no breakdown in that chain.
- **The security evidence was thin.** He said the rule's lead example rested on a footnote to a Fox News article, and that nothing in the rule links a four-year cap to stopping any of the incidents.
- **Cheaper alternatives existed.** Give students the length of their program instead of a cap. DHS's "uniform four years" reason fails because the rule is already a cap tied to program length.
- **The harm is happening now.** Declarations from about 15 schools. UMBC said 32 admitted students withdrew and 14 asked to defer, citing the rule. UW-Milwaukee reported applications down 42 percent and enrollment down 38.7 percent. The University of California said it would hire 20 staff. Grinnell's 3-2 engineering program and a law-to-MBA path at Maryland would have become unlawful for international students.
- **An extension denial cannot be appealed.** Hughes called that "absolutely catastrophic" for a student or a journalist.
- **Relief has to be national.** Border officers at more than 300 ports of entry cannot sort students by whether their school is a plaintiff's member.
- **Vacatur is not permanent.** He said it "doesn't mean the government can't try again."
### The government
- **A checkpoint, not a cap.** Celone said the rule is "an administrative checkpoint" after four years, that a timely extension filing keeps a student in status and studying, and that students already here are grandfathered. He compared it to renewing a library book, then said the analogy was "probably not entirely apt." Hughes answered that "international students are just not library books."
- **Fraud and security.** More routine biometrics, plus "pay-to-stay" schemes by diploma mills. SEVIS is "a data-reporting system for schools," and the schools report on themselves. He likened it to taking off shoes after one shoe-bomb attempt.
- **Costs.** An OMB circular does not require agencies to put a dollar value on benefits like national security. Enrollment loss is speculative and "too attenuated," since international enrollment fell 2.7 percent and nonimmigrant visas 10 percent before the rule.
- **Process.** A 32-day comment period is lawful, and 22,000 comments came in.
- **Authority.** 8 U.S.C. 1184(a) lets DHS set the "time and conditions" of admission, and the D.C. Circuit's OPT decision (Washington Alliance) says so.
- **No irreparable harm.** The plaintiffs' claims are conjecture, and money and compliance costs are not irreparable.
- **Scope.** Trump v. CASA bars relief wider than the plaintiffs, although Celone acknowledged "the complications" of a narrow order.
- **Too early for final judgment.** DHS had not yet produced the administrative record.
- **Journalists.** He said 96 percent of I-visa holders do not hold a job past 240 days and called the rule content-neutral.
### What the judge pressed on
- "Can the President just put the label of national security on something and prohibit me from looking behind it?" He counted five episodes involving "10 or 11 people" in the government's brief and said a student who wants to photograph a base "could do that within four years," or on a tourist visa. "I'm struggling to see any real, rational connection."
- On the four-year cap: it "doesn't have anything to do with national security."
- On the 32-day comment period: it is "barely legal," and it suggests DHS was trying to push the rule through at high speed.
- On enrollment: the government's claim that students would not care is "contrary to common sense." He walked through a student who needs a fifth year and asked whether that student would simply go to Sydney or London.
- On the no-appeal extension: it puts a student's fate at "the whim of one person from which no appeal is possible."
- On journalists: he asked whether he could weigh that renewals might be denied over coverage critical of the government.
- On the clock: "September 15th is 12 days away." He floated a short TRO to buy time and said "I know I'm not likely to be the last word on this issue."
## The November 16 Ruling
The final order adopts the September 14 memorandum, a 48-page opinion, because the issues were "virtually identical" and a challenge to a rule has no disputed facts. It adds two things.
1. **Arbitrary and capricious, carried over from September.**
	DHS did not weigh costs and benefits, its fraud and security reasons did not support the rule, it ignored less burdensome alternatives, and it did not answer significant comments. The September opinion was sharper than the final order. It said the national security case "borders on the absurd," called the comment period "barely legal," and said the weak fit between rule and reasons "raises legitimate questions" about whether the real aim was "asserting greater government control over academic institutions and the press." It also said a court may not block a rule for being unwise, only for breaking the law.
2. **New: DHS never weighed using SEVIS better.**
	DHS argued SEVIS is "a data-reporting system for schools, not an immigration-status adjudication system." The court found DHS never explained how "an unreviewable, completely discretionary renewal system" after four years helps catch fraud, or why that beats "the immense costs of abandoning the existing system."
3. **New: the academic limits exceed DHS's power.**
	Using the Washington Alliance test, DHS may set the "time" and "conditions" of stay only in ways "reasonably related to the nature and purpose of the F-1 visa class," and that purpose "is to permit, and indeed encourage, foreign students to pursue educational opportunities." Barring Ph.D.-length study, joint degrees, changes of concentration, and a second degree at the same level, such as an M.B.A. after a J.D., fails that test. The court wrote that DHS "is seeking to prevent or discourage the very activity" Congress "sought to permit and encourage," and that "the statute simply does not grant DHS such broad and open-ended authority to undermine the F-1 visa program."
4. **Remedy: vacatur, not a remand.**
	DHS asked for a remand without vacatur. The court said that is for cases where vacating would be "quite disruptive," and "this is not such a case. The current regime has existed for decades, and the Final Rule has not gone into effect."
What it did not decide: the statutory holding covers F-1 academic limits. It does not clearly say DHS can never set a fixed admission period, and the J and I parts of the rule fell on the APA grounds alone, per Immigration Analytics' reading of the order. The order also does not discuss limiting the relief to the plaintiffs' members. The September memo had reasoned that APA relief is not limited to the parties, citing the First Circuit's Woonasquatucket decision, and Hughes argued the same at the hearing.
## What Happens Next
1. **There is no single final-verdict date.**
	The trial court's final judgment is done. What follows depends on what the government chooses to do, and each step has its own clock.
2. **The appeal deadline is about January 15, 2027.**
	The government gets 60 days to appeal. Immigration Analytics counts January 15 if judgment was entered November 16. I have not seen the separate judgment entry, so the exact date is unconfirmed. Fragomen and Boston University's ISSO expect an appeal, and Nixon Peabody says DHS may appeal. I found none filed as of November 18.
3. **An appeal alone does not bring the rule back.**
	The Presidents' Alliance says filing an appeal or asking for a stay "does not itself suspend the ruling." The rule returns only if a court grants a stay pending appeal, first asked of Judge Saylor and then of the First Circuit, or reverses him. My tracker shows that the First Circuit denied a stay in the $100,000 H-1B case on July 24, but that is a different case and not a forecast.
4. **The older appeal is likely moot.**
	No. 26-2112 challenges the September order, which the final judgment replaced. It is on hold until about December 13. The government can also appeal the final judgment, and the two could be combined.
5. **DHS can write a new rule.**
	Vacatur does not stop that. Immigration Analytics argues the statutory holding matters most, because "better reasoning on remand cannot cure a lack of authority," so a new rule would have to drop the academic limits and answer the SEVIS and cost questions. The first rule took about 11 months from proposal to final (August 28, 2025 to July 17, 2026), so a replacement at that pace would not reach me before late 2027. That timing is my arithmetic, not a source's claim.
6. **DHS's posture is hostile.**
	DHS's general counsel, James Percival, posted that "Come on a student visa, take one class per semester, and stay here for decades. According to Judge Dennis Saylor, ICE must permit this rampant abuse of our immigration system." Nixon Peabody also warns that DHS has "signaled an increased focus on maintenance of status," so expect tougher scrutiny when students change to H-1B or other statuses, whatever happens to this rule.
7. **It could go higher.**
	After the First Circuit the losing side could seek Supreme Court review. No source gives timing. I would plan on months, not weeks.
Nixon Peabody put the open question plainly: whether schools "have secured a lasting victory or merely won this round."
## What Is Wrong On The Internet
- The eCFR "current" view of 8 CFR 214.2 prints the postponed rule's wording, including a 30-day OPT filing window, so it misstated the operative law before the ruling. The version dated July 1 shows 60 days. I have not rechecked it since November 16.
- Johns Hopkins's F-1 resource page, updated October 12, describes the rule's 30-day windows as if they apply, with an update banner noting the injunction. Other pages, like USCIS's OPT and I-765 pages and a law firm's 14-month-rule article, still give 60 days.
- ISSS's duration of status hub was last updated September 15 and, when I checked on November 18, still said a lawsuit "paused" the rule and that its F and J pages are "only valid until the new rule goes into effect." That is one step behind: the rule is vacated, not paused. Its FAQ, updated October 14, still says the rule "goes into effect on September 15." Its March 18, 2027 cutoff for the OPT filing exemption was correct, since the date is written into the rule, but that date now matters only if the rule returns. ISSS's student email (received by November 19) says the rule "continues to not be in effect" and that ISSS will watch for an appeal and post updates on the hub, but the hub still showed its September 15 text when I rechecked it on November 19.
- The Presidents' Alliance litigation page still lists an in-person hearing on September 9 in its history. The docket and the transcript say September 3.
- Social posts saying "the 4-year limit is on hold" understate it. The rule is vacated. Posts that skip the appeal risk overstate it.
- Some pages and comments read "the D/S rule has ended" backwards. One r/f1visa comment assumed students now have a fixed stay period. The rule that would have ended D/S was cancelled, so D/S continues. A Nolo page on 2026 updates still describes the fixed-date system as coming, and so is stale too.
## What It Means For Me
This section is analysis.
- *Now:* open-ended admission and the 60-day OPT windows apply, as before the rule. I have nothing to file, and no extension-of-stay form applies. The step-by-step route from here to graduation and OPT is in [[VISA Plan]].
- *If the vacatur holds through appeal:* nothing changes through my 2028 graduation.
- *If the government wins a stay or a reversal before 2028:* I would be a current student under D/S on the effective date, so I could stay until my program end date plus 60 days. But the no-extension exemption for OPT filers ends March 18, 2027 as written, and I would file in early 2028. Unless DHS extends that date, I should expect to file an extension of stay with the OPT application, with its fee and biometrics, and the OPT filing and start windows would shrink to 30 days.
- *If DHS writes a new rule:* it needs a new proposal, comment period, and final rule. The earlier cycle took about 11 months, so a replacement is unlikely to bind me before late 2027.
- *Travel:* with the rule vacated, leaving and returning gets the normal D/S admission. It becomes a trap again only if the rule returns, because a re-entry after that date produces a fixed admission and an extension-of-stay requirement for OPT. I will check the I-94 on each entry.
- *Status scrutiny:* whatever happens in court, DHS is looking harder at whether students maintained status. I should keep full enrollment, report address changes, and not let anything lapse before a change of status ([[F-1 Visa]]).
- *The OPT fee rule assumed it:* the fee notice was drafted as if the fixed-period rule is part of the baseline. The vacatur removes that assumption, but the fee proposal itself is untouched ([[OPT Fee Proposal]]).
## Words Used In This Note
- *Duration of status (D/S):* the open-ended stay F-1 students have now.
- *Admit Until Date:* the fixed end date the new rule would put on the I-94.
- *Extension of stay:* an application to USCIS for more time.
- *Injunction:* a court order that bars the government from doing something.
- *Postponement (section 705):* a court delays a rule's start date while a case continues.
- *Summary judgment:* a final decision on the law when no facts are in dispute.
- *Vacatur (vacate):* a court cancels a rule entirely. It is the usual remedy for an unlawful rule.
- *Appeal:* asking a higher court to review a ruling.
- *Stay pending appeal:* a request that a ruling not take effect while the loser appeals.
- *Abeyance:* a pause in an appeal.
