# Duration of Status Rule
In July 2026 DHS finalized a rule that would end the open-ended admission F-1 students have had since 1978 and replace it with a fixed date, and a federal court postponed it the day before it took effect. It is the rule most likely to rearrange the dates in my own timeline if it comes back. This note captures what the rule says, why DHS wrote it, how the transition works, where the lawsuit stands, and which pages are wrong about it. Read it whenever a page quotes a 30-day window.

## What Duration Of Status Was
==Duration of status meant an F-1 student was admitted with no end date and could stay for as long as they kept studying or doing authorized practical training, plus a grace period.== The I-94 read "D/S." The fixed-period rule changes that so every F admission carries an Admit Until Date, and anyone needing more time must ask for it.
DHS's reasons are in the rule's executive summary. F admissions were over 1.8 million in fiscal 2024, up more than 11 percent from 2023 and far above roughly 260,000 in the 1980-81 school year. DHS says D/S does not give officers enough chances to verify that students are doing only what the status allows, it found over 2,100 people who first entered as F-1 students between 2000 and 2010 still in active F-1 status as of April 2025, and it points to the post-9/11 push to track students through SEVIS. It concludes that admission for D/S "is not appropriate" and returns F-1 to the practice before 1979 ([[SEVP and DSOs]]).
## What The Rule Changes For F Students
1. **A fixed admission period**
	Admission for up to the program length on the I-20, not more than four years, plus 30 days to arrive and 30 days to depart. English language training is limited to 24 months. Public high school is limited to 12 months in total.
2. **Extension of stay**
	A student needing more time to finish, start a new program, or do post-completion OPT or STEM OPT must file Form I-539 with USCIS, with the fee and biometrics, after a DSO recommends it in SEVIS. Alternatively the student can leave and be readmitted by CBP with a new period. DHS recommends applying 180 days before the date expires.
3. **OPT dates**
	During OPT the Admit Until Date is the end date on the work permit plus 30 days to depart.
4. **Departure period**
	30 days after the program or OPT ends, down from 60.
5. **Working during a pending extension**
	A timely filing before the 30-day departure period lets a student keep on-campus work, hardship work, and CPT for up to 240 days. Filing during the departure period means all work stops until approval.
6. **Academic mobility limits**
	Below the graduate level, no change of educational level, major, or school in the first year without an SEVP exception. At the graduate level and above, none at any point in the program. After finishing, no enrolling in a program at the same or a lower level.
## The Transition For Current Students
- A student in the country under D/S on the effective date may stay without an extension until the program end date or the end of valid OPT or STEM OPT, not exceeding four years, plus 60 days to depart.
- A student already in the country under D/S who files for post-completion OPT or STEM OPT on or before **March 18, 2027** needs no extension of stay. DHS's FAQ calls this "within six months of the effective date," but the rule text fixes the calendar date, and says the date may be extended if DHS decides it is necessary.
- A student who leaves and is readmitted gets a fixed period, and must then file an extension of stay together with the OPT application.
- A student with a timely cap-subject H-1B petition who qualifies for cap-gap needs no extension and stays in F-1 until approval or April 1 ([[Cap-Gap Extension]]).
## Where The Lawsuit Stands
1. **August 18**
	NAFSA, the Presidents' Alliance, AICUM, two teachers' and graduate-employee unions, the UAW and its local, and the NewsGuild-CWA sued in the District of Massachusetts, No. 1:26-cv-13799, before Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV.
2. **September 3 and 14**
	After a hearing, the judge on September 14 granted relief under section 705 of the Administrative Procedure Act, which postpones the effective date. He found the plaintiffs likely to succeed on arbitrary and capricious, and the docket calls the order a preliminary injunction postponing the rule. His order denied vacatur and summary judgment "without prejudice to its renewal."
3. **September 30 to October 19**
	The government filed notice of appeal on September 30. At an October 2 conference it said it would ask the First Circuit to expedite and wanted district proceedings paused. The government's assented motion to expedite was ruled on October 9, and on October 19 the First Circuit set an expedited schedule with the government's brief due November 9. In the entries I read the government asked only to expedite and did not seek to lift the order.
4. **The district schedule**
	The administrative record is due October 23, supplemental summary-judgment briefs October 26, and replies November 2, according to the Presidents' Alliance. The plaintiffs want a prompt final judgment so the issues travel together.
The appeal does not revive the rule. It stays blocked while the litigation continues, so the 60-day rules are the operative ones ([[Visa Litigation Tracker]]).
## What Is Wrong On The Internet
- The eCFR "current" view of 8 CFR 214.2 prints the postponed rule's wording, including a 30-day OPT filing window, so it misstates the operative law. The version dated July 1 shows 60 days.
- Johns Hopkins's F-1 resource page, updated October 12, describes the rule's 30-day windows as if they apply, with an update banner noting the injunction. A later line on the same page still says there is "no indication" the effective date will change. Other pages, like USCIS's OPT and I-765 pages and a law firm's 14-month-rule article, still give 60 days.
- ISSS's duration of status FAQ, updated October 14, still says the rule "goes into effect on September 15," while its hub banner says a lawsuit paused it. Its March 18, 2027 cutoff for the OPT filing exemption is correct, since that date is written into the rule. DHS's "six months" is a paraphrase of it.
## What It Means For Me
This section is analysis.
- *If the postponement holds:* nothing changes, and the 60-day rules and open-ended admission apply through my 2028 graduation.
- *If the rule comes back before 2028:* I would be a current student under D/S on the effective date, so I could stay until my program end date plus 60 days. But the no-extension exemption for OPT filers ends on March 18, 2027 as written, and I would file in early 2028. Unless DHS extends that date, I should expect to file an extension of stay with the OPT application, with its fee and biometrics, and the OPT filing and start windows would shrink to 30 days.
- *Travel is a trap:* leaving after the rule takes effect produces a fixed admission and an extension-of-stay requirement for OPT.
- *The OPT fee rule assumes it:* the fee notice is drafted as if the fixed-period rule is part of the baseline, so a revived rule and a final fee rule would stack ([[OPT Fee Proposal]]).
