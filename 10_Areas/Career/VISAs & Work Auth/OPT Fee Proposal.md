# OPT Fee Proposal
On October 8, 2026 ICE proposed charging schools $70,000 for a student's first OPT and $30,000 for each later one. It is the largest threat to the plan I am building, because I will ask for OPT in early 2028 and a final rule could be in force by then. This note captures the proposal from the Federal Register text: what it says, how DHS justifies it, the numbers it uses, what it admits, and how to comment. Read it before the November 9 comment deadline and whenever a final rule is announced.
## In Plain Words
The government has proposed a new charge for OPT. It would not be billed to me. The school would pay $70,000 the first time it recommends a student for any OPT and $30,000 for each later recommendation, and the school's international office could not even enter the recommendation until it was paid. The proposal does not say the school must absorb the cost, so UMN could pay it, charge it to students or employers, or stop recommending OPT.
A "proposal" is not a rule yet. The ISSS advisor walked me through what happens next: there is a comment period that ends November 9, the government must read and answer the comments, and only then does it publish a final rule that usually starts about 60 days later. She does not expect fast action, because the end-of-duration-of-status rule took "almost a year." I checked: from proposal to final it took 323 days, while the weighted H-1B lottery rule took 96. Her advice was to focus on the application process and decide when I am applying ([[ISSS October Meeting Brief]], [[US Immigration Rules Explained]]).

## What The Rule Would Do
==The fee attaches to the school's act of recommending OPT, not to the employer or the job, so a student could owe nothing personally and still be priced out by the school's decision.== The $70,000 applies the first time a school recommends a student for any type of OPT. The $30,000 applies to any later recommendation for a student who has paid the first, whether another pre-completion OPT, a post-completion OPT, or a STEM extension. Changing employers does not trigger a new fee because fees follow recommendations.
- *Gating:* a DSO cannot enter the OPT recommendation in SEVIS until the fee is paid and must confirm payment before the student applies to USCIS. USCIS would not grant employment authorization if the school has not paid.
- *Refunds:* a school may request a refund only before the student receives an EAD, after removing the SEVIS recommendation. Refunds are at SEVP's discretion, final, and not appealable. After the EAD issues, no refund.
- *Money flow:* fees go to the U.S. Treasury as miscellaneous receipts. ICE cannot keep them.
- *Scope:* OPT only. The notice says it "does not address CPT."
- *Alternative:* DHS asks for comment on charging the $70,000 again for the first OPT at each higher degree level, so a bachelor's then master's student would pay it twice.
## Who Really Pays
The rule does not decide who bears the cost. DHS says it does not "propose the imposition of specific limitations" on how schools source the money and "acknowledges that the schools may pass the financial obligation of this proposed fee onto F-1 nonimmigrant students, all students, or employers." It says such pass-through would "further discourage misuse of OPT." So a school may absorb it, bill the student, bill the employer, spread it across all tuition, or decline to recommend.
DHS's own model has two cases. In the high-participation case schools cover every fee. In the low-participation case "DSOs recommend only the prospective STEM OPT" students and non-STEM students lose OPT. A computer science major sits in the group DHS expects schools to protect, which is encouraging but not a guarantee.
## DHS's Case
1. **Fraud and abuse**
	The notice cites an HSI investigation, announced May 2026, that it says identified more than 10,000 students working for "highly suspect employers": empty buildings, residential addresses listed as worksites for hundreds of students, "phantom employees," and shell companies. It cites a 2020 conviction of a businesswoman who supplied false employment documents to at least 2,693 students, and ICE site visits in Northern Virginia in October 2025.
2. **Scale beyond oversight**
	194,554 students had OPT work permits and reported employment in 2024, up from 160,627 in 2023, and 165,524 were on STEM OPT. Roughly 6,186 certified schools are covered by about 60 field representatives. DHS says "the gates have opened too widely."
3. **Protecting American workers**
	DHS cites the September 2025 H-1B proclamation's unemployment figures for recent computer science and computer engineering graduates, the payroll tax exemption for F-1 workers, and a 2017 study on wages. It says OPT lets students "circumvent employment visa caps."
4. **Why a fee, not rules**
	DHS considered extending STEM OPT's site visits, training plans and reporting to all OPT and rejected it as slower and dependent on self-attestation. It says a fee "can be implemented quickly and uniformly" and creates "a tangible stake in the process."
5. **Why these amounts**
	DHS chose an initial amount "comparable to the fee for H-1B visas," designed to align with them "to reduce students and employers from using OPT to circumvent the H-1B visa program fees," and says the amounts are on par with a year or two of tuition. The higher first fee reflects that students at first OPT may not have been vetted since entry.
6. **A warning to the program**
	"DHS notes that without the fees proposed in this rule, it cannot operate OPT consistent with its focus on preventing fraud and may shut down the program entirely."
7. **Reliance**
	DHS says it considered reliance and concludes "the national interest in protecting American workers and program integrity far outweighs any reliance interests of foreign students, employers... or educational institutions." The rule would apply to students who apply for OPT after the effective date.
## The Numbers DHS Uses
DHS estimates annual fee payments between $8.4 billion and $16.5 billion, with a primary estimate of $12.4 billion, and 10-year transfers of $74.6 billion to $144.3 billion. It estimates annualized costs of $32 million to $8.2 billion, with a primary estimate of $4.1 billion, including a lost-OPT opportunity cost it measures by the fees that go unpaid. It estimates that 56 percent of certified schools are small entities and says it considered exempting them but rejected that because it "could leave significant fraud vulnerabilities unaddressed." It acknowledges that schools unable or unwilling to pay could see lower enrollment and revenue.
## What DHS Signals Next
The notice calls the fee "a crucial first step" in "a graduated strategy" and says adopting it "should not be interpreted as precluding future changes to the OPT program through additional regulatory requirements." It also says OPT "is not the only practical training program with the potential for significant fraud and national security issues," that fraud and security threats in CPT "have also been historically prevalent," and that DHS "will continue to monitor" them and "take additional actions as necessary." Read together with the August CPT messages, the government's direction on all F-1 practical training is one-way for now ([[CPT Broadcast Messages]]).
## What It Means For Me
- *Timing:* the 60-day delay means the earliest a final rule could bite is spring 2027, and my recommendation request falls in early 2028. Nothing is known about when or whether it is finalized.
- *The sequence matters:* the notice itself prices pre-completion plus post-completion OPT at $100,000, against $70,000 for post-completion alone, and expects institutions to steer students to the latter. Adding a STEM extension makes $130,000 under the per-recommendation rule, my arithmetic ([[Pre-Completion OPT]]).
- *The decider is ISSS:* UMN's choice to pay, pass on, or decline is the variable I cannot see ([[SEVP and DSOs]]).
## When Could It Take Effect
The two recent examples give a range, and they are analogies, not forecasts.
### The Two Analogies
| If it moves like | Final rule about | In force about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The weighted H-1B lottery rule (96 days) | Jan 12, 2027 | Mar 13, 2027 |
| The duration-of-status rule (323 days) | Aug 27, 2027 | Oct 26, 2027 |
### What The Range Means
Both examples land before the Jan 31, 2028 date when I could ask ISSS for my post-graduation OPT I-20, and the fast example lands before a March 2027 pre-completion request. The fee applies to recommendations dated on or after the effective date. So I plan as if the fee could apply, and I watch the rule rather than assume it will stall ([[2028 Graduation Timeline]]).
## How To Comment
Comments are filed on regulations.gov under docket ICEB-2026-0100, must say which section they address and why, and must reach DHS by 11:59 PM Eastern on November 9. Emails and letters are not accepted as comments. Comments become public with whatever personal information is in them. Students can comment on the real effect on access to training and the school's ability to pay, which the notice invites.
## Words Used In This Note
- *Notice of proposed rulemaking:* the government's announcement of a rule it wants to make.
- *Comment period:* the time when the public may write to the government about a proposal.
- *Final rule:* the version that becomes law, with an effective date.
- *Recommendation:* the school official's formal support for my OPT, entered in SEVIS.
- *Pass-through:* the school billing its cost to students or employers.
