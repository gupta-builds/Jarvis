## Chapter Summary
==A learning algorithm improves on a specified task from specified experience under a specified performance measure, and it generalizes only when its capacity and inductive preferences match the available data.==
*Mechanism:* define task $T$, performance $P$, and experience $E$; fit a hypothesis using training examples; evaluate independent test examples; then control capacity so low training error does not become a large generalization gap. An estimator's quality is judged by its bias (systematic error) and variance (sensitivity to resampling); maximum likelihood and Bayesian estimation are the two standard ways to turn data into a parameter estimate, trading a single point estimate for a full posterior distribution. Unsupervised learning extracts a "simpler" representation of $x$ — PCA finds an uncorrelated low-dimensional linear projection, k-means finds a sparse one-hot cluster assignment — and both strategies run into the curse of dimensionality once the feature space grows large enough that most regions of it contain no training example at all.
## Key Concepts
### §5.1 Learning Algorithms
- **Task, performance, experience:** a program learns from experience $E$ on task class $T$ when its performance measure $P$ improves. This prevents an algorithm description from naming only a model while omitting what it must do or how success is measured.
- **Learning tasks:** classification maps features to a category; regression maps features to a real value. The chapter also distinguishes transcription, translation, structured output, anomaly detection, synthesis, imputation, denoising, and density estimation; these are different output or observation structures, not interchangeable labels.
- **Performance measure:** accuracy and $L_{0-1}(\hat y_i,y_i)=\mathbf{1}_{\hat y_i\ne y_i}$ suit classification; density estimation uses probability assigned to held-out examples. Evaluate on independently collected test examples to measure generalization rather than memorization.
- **Experience and design matrix:** supervised experience pairs $x$ with $y$; unsupervised experience supplies $x$ alone. Fixed-size examples can be arranged as $X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with examples in rows and features in columns.
- **Linear regression example:** with $\hat y=w^\top x$, the test loss is $\frac{1}{m_{test}}\|X_{test}w-y_{test}\|_2^2$. The normal-equation form $w=(X_{train}^\top X_{train})^{-1}X_{train}^\top y_{train}$ depends on the stated nonsingularity assumption.
### §5.2 Capacity, Overfitting and Underfitting
- **Generalization gap:** training error is measured on fitted data; generalization error is expected error on new data. Underfitting leaves training error too high, whereas overfitting leaves a large gap between training and test performance.
- **Capacity:** representational capacity is the family a model can express by changing parameters; effective capacity is what the optimization process actually reaches. More parameters alone do not prove more effective capacity.
- **i.i.d. assumption and Bayes error:** standard reasoning assumes examples are independent and the train/test sets have the same data-generating distribution. Bayes error is the oracle floor under the true distribution, not a score obtained by training harder.
- **Regularization:** a modification intended to reduce generalization error without reducing training error. Weight decay adds $\lambda w^\top w$ to the training objective; raising $\lambda$ favors smaller weights and reduces effective capacity.
### §5.4 Estimators, Bias and Variance
- **Point estimator:** any function of the data $\hat\theta_m = g(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)})$ that produces a single "best" guess at a parameter $\theta$ (or a whole function, in which case it's called a function estimator). Nothing in the definition requires $g$'s output to be close to the truth — that's a property to check, not to assume.
- **Bias (§5.4.2):** $\mathrm{bias}(\hat\theta_m)=E[\hat\theta_m]-\theta$. An estimator is **unbiased** if this is exactly zero, **asymptotically unbiased** if it only vanishes as $m\to\infty$. The sample mean is unbiased for both a Bernoulli parameter and a Gaussian mean; the naive sample variance $\hat\sigma_m^2=\frac1m\sum(x^{(i)}-\hat\mu_m)^2$ is **biased** by exactly $-\sigma^2/m$, which is why the corrected estimator divides by $m-1$ instead of $m$.
### §5.5 Maximum Likelihood Estimation
- **Maximum likelihood estimator:** $\theta_{ML}=\arg\max_\theta \prod_i p_{model}(x^{(i)};\theta)$, equivalently $\arg\max_\theta E_{x\sim\hat p_{data}}\log p_{model}(x;\theta)$ after taking logs to turn the product into a sum (and dividing by $m$ to express it as an expectation under the empirical distribution).
- **MLE as KL-divergence minimization:** maximizing likelihood is equivalent to minimizing $D_{KL}(\hat p_{data}\,\|\,p_{model})$, which reduces to minimizing the cross-entropy $-E_{x\sim\hat p_{data}}[\log p_{model}(x)]$ — the term that doesn't depend on the model is thrown away. This is why "minimize cross-entropy" and "maximize likelihood" name the same optimization.
- **Conditional MLE:** for supervised learning, the target is $\theta_{ML}=\arg\max_\theta P(Y\mid X;\theta)$ — mean squared error for linear regression is literally the cross-entropy between the empirical distribution and a Gaussian model, which is why "minimize MSE" and "maximize Gaussian likelihood" are the same procedure.
### §5.6 Bayesian Statistics
- **Frequentist vs. Bayesian:** frequentist statistics treats the true $\theta$ as fixed-but-unknown and the estimate $\hat\theta$ as the random variable (since it's a function of random data). Bayesian statistics flips this: the observed data is fixed, and $\theta$ itself is a random variable with a **prior** $p(\theta)$ representing degree-of-belief before seeing data.
- **Posterior update:** $p(\theta\mid x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)}) \propto p(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)$ via Bayes' rule — observing data narrows a broad (high-entropy) prior into a posterior concentrated around a few likely values.
- **Full-distribution prediction:** rather than plugging in one point estimate, Bayesian prediction integrates over every possible $\theta$, weighted by its posterior probability: $p(x^{(m+1)}\mid x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)}) = \int p(x^{(m+1)}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)})\,d\theta$. This integral is what protects against overfitting when training data is limited — the method doesn't commit to a single possibly-wrong $\theta$.
### §5.8 Unsupervised Learning Algorithms
- **"Best" representation:** unsupervised learning looks for a representation of $x$ that keeps as much information as possible while satisfying a simplicity constraint — lower-dimensional, sparse, or statistically independent (not mutually exclusive: dimensionality reduction often removes redundancy, which is itself a step toward independence).
- **PCA (§5.8.1) as decorrelation:** PCA learns an orthogonal linear map $z=xW$ chosen so $\mathrm{Var}[z]$ is diagonal — i.e. the output coordinates are mutually uncorrelated. The derivation goes through the SVD of the (mean-centered) design matrix $X=U\Lambda W^\top$: since $X^\top X=W\Lambda^2W^\top$, projecting with $W$ (the right singular vectors) guarantees $\mathrm{Var}[z]=\frac{1}{m-1}\Lambda^2$, a diagonal matrix.
- **k-means (§5.8.2) as a sparse code:** k-means represents $x$ with a $k$-dimensional **one-hot** code $h$ (one entry is 1, the rest are 0) rather than a continuous vector — an extreme case of sparse representation. The algorithm alternates: assign each point to its nearest centroid, then recompute each centroid as the mean of its assigned points, until assignments stop changing.
- **Clustering is ill-posed:** there is no single ground-truth criterion for "correct" clusters — a dataset of red/gray cars/trucks can be validly split by color, by vehicle type, or into four finer groups, and none of these is objectively "more correct" without an external task to judge against.
### §5.11.1 The Curse of Dimensionality
- **Exponential blow-up in configurations:** with $d$ dimensions and $v$ distinguishable values per axis, the number of distinct regions of the input space grows as $O(v^d)$ — 10 regions in 1D, 100 in 2D, 1000 in 3D, for the same $v=10$. Past a modest $d$, almost every region has zero training examples in it, so "look at nearby training points" (the implicit strategy behind kNN, histograms, and most classical ML) stops being informative.
## Full Reading Notes
### §5.4 Estimators, Bias and Variance (p. 122)
Point estimation (§5.4.1, p. 122) is extended via **function estimation** — estimating a whole function $\hat f$ rather than a scalar/vector $\theta$ is treated as "just" point estimation in function space, which is why the linear-regression weight vector $w$ and a general function estimate $\hat f$ get the same bias/variance machinery. §5.4.2 (p. 124) walks three worked derivations: the Bernoulli mean estimator $\hat\theta_m=\frac1m\sum x^{(i)}$ is shown unbiased by direct expectation algebra; the Gaussian sample mean $\hat\mu_m$ is shown unbiased the same way; and the two variance estimators are directly contrasted — $\hat\sigma_m^2$ (divide by $m$) has bias $-\sigma^2/m$, while $\tilde\sigma_m^2$ (divide by $m-1$, "Bessel's correction") is exactly unbiased. The chapter's own closing point: unbiasedness is desirable but not sufficient — biased estimators are routinely preferred for other properties (lower variance, consistency).
### §5.5 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (p. 131)
The chapter derives $\theta_{ML}$ from the product-likelihood, log-transforms it for numerical stability (avoiding underflow from multiplying many small probabilities), then reframes the whole thing as minimizing $D_{KL}(\hat p_{data}\|p_{model})$. The key move: the $\hat p_{data}$ term in the KL divergence doesn't involve the model, so minimizing the full KL divergence and minimizing just the cross-entropy term are the same optimization problem — this is the formal justification for why "negative log-likelihood" is the name used for loss functions throughout deep learning (p. 132), and why MSE for Gaussian-output regression is a cross-entropy in disguise (§5.5.1, p. 132).
### §5.6 Bayesian Statistics (p. 135)
Contrasts with §5.4's frequentist framing directly: frequentist treats $\theta$ as fixed and $\hat\theta$ as random (a function of random data); Bayesian treats the *data* as fixed/observed and $\theta$ as the random variable, governed by a prior $p(\theta)$ that is deliberately chosen broad/high-entropy before any data arrives. The worked example (p. 136, Bayesian linear regression) sets up $p(y^{(train)}\mid X^{(train)},w)=\mathcal N(y^{(train)};X^{(train)}w,I)$ and begins specifying a prior over $w$ — the chapter's point being that Bayesian estimation generalizes better with little data (because it never commits to one $\theta$) but costs more computationally once data is abundant.
### §5.8 Unsupervised Learning Algorithms (p. 145)
After the three-way "simpler representation" framing (low-dimensional / sparse / independent), §5.8.1 (p. 146) re-derives PCA via the SVD rather than the eigendecomposition route used in prior chapters — the point of showing both is that $X^\top X=W\Lambda^2W^\top$ connects the eigenvector view to the SVD view directly. §5.8.2 (p. 149) gives k-means' two-step iteration and then spends a full page (the red-cars/gray-trucks example) making the "clustering has no single right answer" point concrete before closing into the practical issue of *how many* clusters to even ask for.
### §5.11.1 The Curse of Dimensionality (p. 154)
Figure 5.9 is the chapter's own illustration: 1 relevant dimension with 10 regions of interest is easy to cover with examples; 2 dimensions needs $10\times10=100$ regions; 3 needs $10^3=1000$. The chapter frames this explicitly as a *statistical* challenge (distinct from the *computational* curse-of-dimensionality framing used elsewhere) — in high dimensions, the number of possible input configurations vastly exceeds the number of available training examples, so the classic "nearest-point" generalization strategy that works fine in low dimensions runs out of nearby points to generalize from.
## Worked Example
- The Iris data example arranges 150 plants and four measurements as $X\in\mathbb{R}^{150\times4}$, making the row/example and column/feature convention tangible.
- A quadratic data-generating process illustrates capacity: degree one underfits, degree two can match the structure, and degree nine can interpolate training points while oscillating between them.
- Applying weight decay to the high-degree polynomial suppresses large coefficients; extreme regularization flattens the model, while too little permits the original overfit behavior.
- The Bernoulli-mean bias derivation (p. 124) is a full, short algebraic chain: $\mathrm{bias}(\hat\theta_m)=E[\frac1m\sum x^{(i)}]-\theta = \frac1m\sum\theta-\theta=0$ — worth reproducing by hand once, since the Gaussian-mean derivation (p. 125) is structurally identical and the variance-estimator derivation (p. 126) is the same technique applied to a case where the answer comes out *nonzero* ($-\sigma^2/m$).
- PCA's SVD derivation (p. 147-148) is a genuine worked proof, not just an assertion: starting from $X=U\Lambda W^\top$, it shows step by step that $\mathrm{Var}[z]=\frac{1}{m-1}\Lambda^2$ using only $U^\top U=I$ and $W^\top W=I$ — this is the same orthogonality argument [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 4|CSCI 5304's SVD lecture]] builds from, applied here to justify why PCA decorrelates rather than just "compresses."
- The curse-of-dimensionality figure's numbers ($10, 100, 1000$ regions for $d=1,2,3$ at $v=10$) are worth keeping memorized as the canonical illustration — they are the exact shape of the argument ISL Chapter 4.4 ([[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 4521/Textbook/ISL/Chapter - 4|ISL Chapter 4]]) makes when explaining why QDA's $K\cdot p(p+1)/2$ covariance parameters become a liability as $p$ grows.
