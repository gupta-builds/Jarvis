- State what the elbow method is for, and why neither PCA's explained-variance ratio nor k-means' within-cluster variation can, by themselves, pick the "right" number of components/clusters.
- Explain, with a concrete scenario, what data leakage is and why PCA must be fit only on training data.
- Contrast Word2Vec, GloVe, and BERT by what each one actually optimizes, per [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 4521/Textbook/ENLP/Chapter - 3|ENLP Chapter 3]] and [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 4521/Textbook/ENLP/Chapter - 6|ENLP Chapter 6]].
## Key ideas (short)
- **k-means in one sentence:** randomly assign, compute centroids, reassign to the nearest centroid, repeat until stable — guaranteed to decrease the objective each step, never guaranteed to find the global optimum.
- **PCA's two equivalent derivations:** maximize sample variance of $Xc$ subject to $\|c\|=1$ (statistics framing) **=** find the eigenvectors of $XX^\top$ ordered by eigenvalue (linear algebra framing) **=** find the right singular vectors of $X$ via SVD — three descriptions of the identical procedure.
- **The elbow method is a heuristic, not a formula:** both PCA's cumulative proportion-of-variance-explained and k-means' within-cluster variation will always "improve" with more components/clusters, so there's no single optimum to solve for — you look for where the improvement curve visibly slows down.
- **Data leakage, concretely:** fitting PCA (or any normalization) on the full dataset before splitting train/test lets test-set information influence the "training" transformation — always split first, then fit PCA (or any such transform) on training data only.
- **Word2Vec → GloVe → BERT is a real historical progression:** Word2Vec (2013) predicts context from a word or vice versa; GloVe (2014) explicitly fits word-vector dot products to log co-occurrence counts; BERT (2018) replaces a fixed word vector with a sentence-context-dependent one via Masked Language Modeling, at the cost of 110-340 million parameters.

### 1. LEC 2.3 — Unsupervised Learning, K-Means Clustering (Tue 10/13)
	Introduces unsupervised learning by direct contrast with supervised learning: same feature observations $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb{R}^p$, but no response vector $y$. Names the course's two unsupervised paradigms: dimensionality reduction (PCA, next lecture — "review your linear algebra notes before next class!!") and clustering (today).
	Defines clustering as grouping unlabeled data by similarity, with similarity operationalized as distance. Gives the full k-means algorithm: choose $k$; randomly assign all points a cluster 1..$k$; iterate (compute each cluster's centroid; reassign each point to its nearest centroid) until assignments stop changing — stating directly that this only reaches a local, not global, minimum.
	Covers implementation choices explicitly: initialization options (random, density-based estimate, or multiple random restarts — "k-means++"); stopping criteria (max iterations, or change-rate below a threshold); and, for choosing $k$, running the algorithm at different $k$ values and evaluating via nearest-neighbor performance.
	Gives the formal within-cluster variation objective and the two defining partition constraints ($\bigcup_i C_i=\{1,\dots,n\}$; $C_i\cap C_j=\emptyset$), states k-means is a (local) minimization of total within-cluster variation, and that it's "guaranteed to keep decreasing the value... with each iteration" but may stop at a local, not global, minimum — hence running it multiple times and keeping the best result.
	States directly that choosing $k$ is hard because there's no train/test split available (no labels to validate against) and within-cluster variation always improves with more clusters — naming the Elbow Method as a heuristic fix, "to be covered in a future class."
### 2. LEC 2.4 — Principal Component Analysis (Tue 10/13, same day as LEC 2.3)
	Opens from the curse of dimensionality (data needed to generalize grows exponentially with dimension) and motivates PCA as a better alternative to manual pairwise-feature-plot inspection when many or all features matter.
	Derives PC1 as the direction of largest sample variance: for centered data, find coefficients $c_1,\dots,c_p$ (with $\sum c_i^2=1$) maximizing $\mathrm{var}(Z)=E[Z^2]$ where $Z=\sum c_iX_i$ — then states this maximization, $\max_c\|Xc\|^2$ subject to $\|c\|=1$, is solved by the first singular row vector of $X$ (equivalently, the top eigenvector of $XX^\top$). Subsequent components are the next singular/eigen-vectors, each orthogonal to all previous ones.
	Gives two equivalent PCA algorithms directly: the "statistical" version (center data, fit a multivariate Gaussian, pick axes of largest variance in sequence) and the "linear algebra" version (center data, compute eigenvectors of $XX^\top$, order by eigenvalue, keep the top $k$) — explicitly noting $XX^\top$ is the data's covariance matrix once centered.
	States PCA's practical applications (denoising, reducing model variance, understanding feature relationships, reducing data needs, visualization) then introduces the proportion of variance explained (PVE) per component and `pca.explained_variance_ratio_`, followed by the elbow test for choosing how many components to keep (see Examples worth keeping).
	Covers data leakage explicitly and generally (duplicate points split across train/test, normalizing before the split, temporal leakage), then specifically for PCA: fit only on training data, never on the full dataset including test — flagged directly as "a common interview question."
### 3. LEC 2.5 — Modern Text Embedding (Thu 10/15)
	Opens with specific tips for HW2 (start early given dataset size; use a 10-20% sample for an initial elbow-method ballpark across $k=2$ to $802$; tune `min_df` and `n_init` for speed; problem 2 is easier with many small clusters; the review-score-threshold requirement is simplified to above/below 2.5).
	Recaps PCA (Last time slide, mirrors LEC 2.4's summary) then states today's content: alternatives to the prior vectorization methods (bag-of-words, tf-idf), motivated by those methods' shared shortcomings — ignoring word meaning, order-independence, high dimensionality (tf-idf adds that rarity isn't always importance).
	Introduces Word2Vec (Mikolov et al., Google, 2013) as a 2-layer neural network mapping words into a space where similar words live close together, via two architectures: CBOW (average context words, predict the middle word) and Skip-gram (one input word, predict each surrounding position) — noting CBOW trains faster but Skip-gram handles rare/unseen words and scales to new languages less easily either way.
	Introduces GloVe (Pennington et al., Stanford, 2014) as combining global co-occurrence statistics (like tf-idf) with local context (like Word2Vec), trained on 6 billion words, seeking a linear relationship between word-pair co-occurrence ratios.
	Introduces BERT (Devlin et al., Google, 2018) as a transformer-based model (110M-340M parameters) trained on two tasks: masked-word-filling ("my dog is ___") and next-sentence prediction ("my dog is cute" → does "he likes playing" or "what's a magnet" follow?).
	Closes with a full three-part Unit 2 recap (NLP fundamentals, k-means, PCA, and general cross-method questions like "K-Means vs. KNN" and combining PCA/k-means/kNN) — directly useful as a self-check list ahead of Quiz 3.

## Lecture-to-textbook synthesis
==PCA and k-means both compress unlabeled data, but by genuinely different criteria (maximum retained variance vs. minimum within-group distance) — and in both cases, the professor's own worked examples (the elbow test, the six-run local-optima comparison) demonstrate that "how many components/clusters" has no formula answer, only a heuristic one.==
*Mechanism:* PCA solves $\max_c\|Xc\|^2$ s.t. $\|c\|=1$ via the eigendecomposition of $XX^\top$ (equivalently SVD), producing a ranked sequence of orthogonal directions; k-means solves $\min_{C_1,\dots,C_k}\sum_k W(C_k)$ via iterative centroid-reassignment, producing a one-hot cluster label per point. Both objectives trivially "improve" by adding more components/clusters, which is exactly why neither can be optimized by just checking the objective value — the elbow method substitutes a visual slope-change heuristic for an actual stopping rule.
- Lecture example/scenario: LEC 2.4's elbow-test figure (cumulative PVE flattening around PC3; within-cluster variation flattening around 8 clusters) is the same heuristic applied to two structurally different objectives.
- Textbook connection: [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 4521/Textbook/ISL/Chapter - 12|ISL Chapter 12]]'s own chapter-level framing ("PCA looks to find a low-dimensional representation... clustering looks to find homogeneous subgroups") is the exact contrast this week's two Tuesday lectures walk through mechanically.

> [!WARNING]
> Assuming PCA's explained-variance ratio or k-means' within-cluster variation has an optimal value to solve for — both metrics monotonically improve with more components/clusters by construction, so "pick the one that maximizes/minimizes this" is not a valid stopping rule.

## Flashcards
Why is k-means only guaranteed to find a local minimum, and what's the standard fix?::Exactly solving the K-means objective would require checking an infeasible number of partitions; the iterative algorithm only guarantees the objective won't increase, so different random initializations can converge to different local optima — the fix is running it multiple times and keeping the best result. #cards/csci4521
What two equivalent ways does the lecture give for computing PCA?::The "statistical" version (fit a multivariate Gaussian, pick axes of largest variance in order) and the "linear algebra" version (compute eigenvectors of the centered data's covariance matrix $XX^\top$, order by eigenvalue, keep the top k) — both give the same answer. #cards/csci4521
Why can't you just pick the number of PCA components or k-means clusters that "maximizes quality"?::Both PCA's explained-variance ratio and k-means' within-cluster variation always improve as you add more components/clusters, so there's no interior optimum — you need a heuristic like the elbow method instead. #cards/csci4521
Why must PCA be fit only on training data, never on the full dataset before a train/test split?::Fitting PCA on the full dataset lets information from the test set influence the transformation applied to the training data — a form of data leakage that inflates apparent performance. #cards/csci4521
What does GloVe explicitly fit that distinguishes it from Word2Vec's training procedure?::GloVe directly optimizes word-vector dot products to approximate the log of word-pair co-occurrence counts; Word2Vec instead learns vectors implicitly through a context-prediction task. #cards/csci4521
