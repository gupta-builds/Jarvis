## Chapter Summary
==Every matrix, with no restriction whatsoever on shape, rank, or determinant, can be written as $A = U\Sigma V^*$, where $U$ and $V$ are unitary and $\Sigma$ is diagonal and non-negative - the single most useful fact in the book.== *Mechanism:* geometrically, the image of the unit sphere under any matrix's action is a **hyperellipse** (p. 25); $U$'s columns are the directions of that hyperellipse's axes, $\Sigma$'s entries are how much each axis got stretched, and $V$'s columns are the pre-image directions that map onto those axes - so the SVD is the algebraic bookkeeping for "which input directions get stretched, by how much, and into which output directions" (p. 25-26).
## Key Concepts
- **Hyperellipse**: the $m$-dimensional generalization of an ellipse, formed by stretching the unit sphere by factors $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m$ (possibly zero) along orthogonal directions $u_1,\ldots,u_m$ (p. 25).
- **Singular values ($\sigma_i$)**: lengths of the $n$ principal semiaxes of $AS$ (the image of the unit sphere $S$), conventionally $\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_n>0$ (p. 26).
- **Left singular vectors ($u_i$)**: unit vectors oriented along the hyperellipse's principal semiaxes (p. 26).
- **Right singular vectors ($v_i$)**: unit vectors in the original unit sphere that are the pre-images of the $u_i$ directions, satisfying $Av_j = \sigma_ju_j$ (p. 26, eq. 4.1).
- **Reduced SVD**: $A = \hat{U}\hat{\Sigma}V^*$ where $\hat U$ is $m\times n$ with orthonormal columns, $\hat\Sigma$ is $n\times n$ diagonal (p. 27, eq. 4.2).
- **Full SVD**: $A = U\Sigma V^*$ where $U$ is $m\times m$ unitary, $\Sigma$ is $m\times n$ diagonal (silent rows/columns included), $V$ is $n\times n$ unitary (p. 27-28, eq. 4.3-4.4).
- **Theorem 4.1** (existence/uniqueness, stated here, proved in [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 5|Lecture 5]]): every matrix has an SVD; singular values are always unique; if square with distinct $\sigma_j$, $U$ and $V$ are unique up to complex sign (p. 29).
- **Induced/operator matrix norm**: $\|A\| = \sup_{x\neq0}\|Ax\|/\|x\|$ (carried over from [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 2 & 3|Lecture 3]], p. 19).

### A geometric observation (p. 25-26)
The SVD is motivated by a single geometric fact, stated but not yet proved here: **the image of the unit sphere under any $m\times n$ matrix is a hyperellipse.** A hyperellipse in $\mathbb{R}^m$ is the surface obtained by stretching the unit sphere by factors $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m$ along orthogonal directions $u_1,\ldots,u_m$ - the vectors $\{\sigma_iu_i\}$ are the **principal semiaxes**. If $A$ has rank $r$, exactly $r$ of the $\sigma_i$ are nonzero. Given this picture, the book *defines* the singular values as the lengths of $AS$'s principal semiaxes (descending order by convention), the left singular vectors as the unit directions of those semiaxes, and the right singular vectors as their pre-images on the original unit sphere, satisfying $Av_j=\sigma_ju_j$ (eq. 4.1). *A real terminology complaint, straight from the book itself (p. 26):* "left" and "right" are awkward names, because in a diagram the left singular vectors correspond to the space drawn on the right, and vice versa - the book admits this is backwards from a sketch's perspective but keeps the convention because it matches where $U$ and $V$ sit in the factorization.
### Reduced SVD (p. 27)
Writing the vector equations $Av_j=\sigma_ju_j$ ($1\leq j\leq n$) as one matrix equation gives $AV = \hat U\hat\Sigma$, or $A = \hat U\hat\Sigma V^*$ (eq. 4.2) - the **reduced SVD**. Here $\hat\Sigma$ is $n\times n$ diagonal with positive entries (assuming full rank $n$), $\hat U$ is $m\times n$ with orthonormal columns, $V$ is $n\times n$ and unitary.
### Full SVD (p. 27-28)
$\hat U$'s $n$ orthonormal columns don't form a basis of $\mathbb{C}^m$ unless $m=n$. Adjoining $m-n$ more orthonormal columns extends $\hat U$ to a unitary $U$; $\hat\Sigma$ is padded with $m-n$ rows of zeros to become $\Sigma$ (an $m\times n$ diagonal matrix), giving the **full SVD** $A=U\Sigma V^*$ (eq. 4.3). The book is explicit that "reduced" vs. "full" and the hatted notation are maintained throughout the book, with the same distinction made later for QR factorizations (p. 27-28) - directly relevant once [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 6|Lecture 6]] and [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 7|Lecture 7]] introduce reduced/full QR. If $A$ is rank-deficient (rank $r<n$), only $r$ of the left singular vectors are determined by the hyperellipse's geometry - $U$ needs $m-r$ arbitrary orthonormal columns, $V$ needs $n-r$, and $\Sigma$ has $r$ positive entries with the rest zero (p. 28).
### Formal definition (p. 28-29)
For arbitrary $m,n$ (no requirement $m\geq n$) and $A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}$ of any rank, an SVD is $A=U\Sigma V^*$ with $U\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}$ unitary, $V\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ unitary, $\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ diagonal with $\sigma_1\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_p\geq0$, $p=\min(m,n)$ (eq. 4.4). $\Sigma$ has the same shape as $A$ even when $A$ isn't square, but $U,V$ are always square. The book notes the logical structure directly: if every matrix has an SVD, then the image of the unit sphere under *any* linear map is a hyperellipse, as claimed at the outset - the existence theorem and the geometric claim are the same statement viewed two ways.
### Existence and uniqueness - Theorem 4.1 (p. 29-31)
Stated here, with the full inductive proof given (not just previewed as the lecture's capture suggested, but actually carried out in the book at this exact point - see the Open Questions note below). *Proof sketch, matching what the professor did in Lecture 5's session almost exactly:* set $\sigma_1=\|A\|_2$; a compactness argument gives unit vectors $v_1,u_1$ with $Av_1=\sigma_1u_1$; extend $v_1,u_1$ to full orthonormal bases via unitary $V_1,U_1$; then $U_1^*AV_1 = S = \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1 & w^*\\0&B\end{bmatrix}$ (eq. 4.5) for some row vector $w$ and smaller matrix $B$; a norm argument forces $w=0$ (identical to the block-decomposition argument [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 5|Lecture 5]]'s note already captures from the live lecture); induct on $B$. *Uniqueness* follows because $\sigma_1=\|A\|_2$ is forced by definition, and if the maximizing $v_1$ weren't unique, the book shows (p. 30-31) this would force $\sigma_1$ to repeat - so a unique $\sigma_1$ implies a unique $v_1,u_1$ up to sign, and the rest follows by induction on the orthogonal complement.

### Contrast with eigenvalue decomposition (from lecture; textbook treats this in Lecture 5)
The professor's contrast - eigendecomposition requires square, requires $m$ independent eigenvectors (can fail/"defective"), eigenvalues can be complex; SVD has none of these restrictions - matches almost word for word the book's own "SVD vs. Eigenvalue Decomposition" section, but that section is written up in [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 5|Lecture 5]]'s text (p. 32-33), not here - another case of the lecture introducing a topic one class before the book's own placement of it.
## Worked Example
*Example 3.1 revisited geometrically (p. 19, 26):* the matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$ from Lecture 3 maps the unit circle to an ellipse; its 2-norm ($\approx 2.9208$) is exactly $\sigma_1$ of its SVD, the length of that ellipse's longest semiaxis - the book explicitly promises "we shall consider how to calculate such 2-norm results in Lecture 5" (p. 19), which is precisely what Theorem 5.3 delivers. *Dough-and-loaf picture (lecture only):* think of the unit sphere as dough; applying $A$ stretches it into a loaf - the longest direction is $u_1$, its length is $\sigma_1$, and the pre-image direction that stretched into it is $v_1$, so $Av_1=\sigma_1u_1$ exactly, and $\|A\|_2=\sigma_1$ falls out of the induced-norm recap directly.
