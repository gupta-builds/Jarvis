## Chapter Summary
==Every matrix looks diagonal once you're willing to change basis on both the input and output sides - $A=U\Sigma V^*$ means $U^*AV=\Sigma$ - and that single fact is why the SVD is useful for almost everything: rank, range, null space, every named matrix norm, and the best possible low-rank approximation all reduce to a statement about the diagonal entries $\sigma_i$ (p. 32-37).== *Mechanism:* the existence proof is inductive - pick off the single largest-stretch direction, prove everything orthogonal to it lands in a strictly smaller, independent sub-problem, then repeat; once the induction bottoms out, every downstream property (rank, range, null space, norms, eigen-relationships, and the best-rank-$\nu$ approximation) falls out algebraically from the same $U,\Sigma,V$.
## Key Concepts
- **Change of basis via the SVD** (p. 32): $b=Ax \iff b'=\Sigma x'$ where $b'=U^*b$, $x'=V^*x$ - $A$ becomes diagonal once the range is expressed in $U$'s basis and the domain in $V$'s.
- **SVD vs. eigendecomposition** (p. 33): the SVD always exists and uses two orthonormal bases; the eigendecomposition $A=X\Lambda X^{-1}$ (eq. 5.1) requires a square, non-defective matrix and generally uses a non-orthogonal basis.
- **Rank via $\Sigma$** (Theorem 5.1, p. 33): $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$, the number of nonzero singular values.
- **Range and null space via $U,V$** (Theorem 5.2, p. 33-34): $\operatorname{range}(A)=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_r\rangle$, $\operatorname{null}(A)=\langle v_{r+1},\ldots,v_n\rangle$.
- **2-norm and Frobenius norm via $\Sigma$** (Theorem 5.3, p. 34): $\|A\|_2=\sigma_1$, $\|A\|_F=\sqrt{\sigma_1^2+\cdots+\sigma_r^2}$.
- **$A^*A$/$AA^*$ eigenvalue relationship** (Theorem 5.4, p. 34): nonzero eigenvalues of $A^*A$ (or $AA^*$) are $\sigma_i^2$.
- **Determinant via $\Sigma$** (Theorem 5.6, p. 35): $|\det A| = \prod_i\sigma_i$ for square $A$.
- **Rank-$\nu$ truncation ($A_\nu$)** (eq. 5.4, p. 35): $A_\nu=\sum_{j=1}^\nu \sigma_ju_jv_j^*$ - the best possible rank-$\nu$ approximation to $A$.
## Full Reading Notes
### A change of bases (p. 32)
Any $b\in\mathbb{C}^m$ expands in $U$'s columns, any $x\in\mathbb{C}^n$ in $V$'s columns; the coordinate vectors are $b'=U^*b$, $x'=V^*x$. Substituting $A=U\Sigma V^*$ into $b=Ax$ gives $U^*b = \Sigma V^*x$, i.e. $b'=\Sigma x'$ (p. 32) - $A$ reduces to the diagonal $\Sigma$ exactly when range and domain are both expressed in their respective singular-vector bases. This is the book's own framing of "every matrix is diagonal, if you pick the right bases," matching the professor's identical phrasing in lecture.
### SVD vs. eigenvalue decomposition (p. 33)
A nondefective square $A=X\Lambda X^{-1}$ (eq. 5.1) diagonalizes $A$ the same way, but with three real differences the book states explicitly: the SVD uses *two* bases (left/right singular vectors), eigendecomposition uses *one* (eigenvectors); SVD's bases are always orthonormal, eigendecomposition's generally isn't; and *every* matrix (even rectangular) has an SVD, while not every square matrix is diagonalizable. The book's own application-level distinction: eigenvalues matter for problems about *iterated* behavior of $A$ (powers $A^k$, exponentials $e^{tA}$); singular vectors matter for problems about $A$ itself or its inverse (p. 33).
### The four core SVD-property theorems (p. 33-34)
*Theorem 5.1 (rank):* $\operatorname{rank}(A)=r$ (number of nonzero $\sigma_i$) - proved because $\operatorname{rank}$ of a diagonal matrix is its nonzero-entry count, and $U,V$ are full rank, so $\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(\Sigma)$. *Theorem 5.2 (range/null space):* $\operatorname{range}(A)=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_r\rangle$, $\operatorname{null}(A)=\langle v_{r+1},\ldots,v_n\rangle$ - a direct consequence of $\operatorname{range}(\Sigma)=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_r\rangle$ and $\operatorname{null}(\Sigma)=\langle e_{r+1},\ldots,e_n\rangle$. *Theorem 5.3 (norms):* $\|A\|_2=\sigma_1$ (already used in Theorem 4.1's proof) and $\|A\|_F=\sqrt{\sigma_1^2+\cdots+\sigma_r^2}$, via Theorem 3.1's unitary-invariance of both norms applied directly to $\Sigma$.
### The $A^*A$/$AA^*$ eigenvalue relationship (Theorem 5.4, p. 34)
$A^*A = V\Sigma^*\Sigma V^* = V(\Sigma^*\Sigma)V^*$ - so $A^*A$ is similar to $\Sigma^*\Sigma$, sharing its $n$ eigenvalues $\sigma_1^2,\ldots,\sigma_p^2$ (plus $n-p$ zeros if $n>p$); symmetrically for $AA^*$'s $m$ eigenvalues. *Theorem 5.5 (p. 34):* if $A=A^*$ (hermitian), the singular values are exactly $|\lambda_i|$ - proved by writing the hermitian eigendecomposition $A=Q\Lambda Q^* = Q|\Lambda|\operatorname{sign}(\Lambda)Q^*$ (eq. 5.2) and noting $\operatorname{sign}(\Lambda)Q^*$ is itself unitary, so this *is* an SVD with singular values $|\lambda_i|$.
### Determinant via singular values (Theorem 5.6, p. 34-35)
For square $A$: $|\det A| = \prod_i\sigma_i$ - because $\det$ of a product is the product of determinants, and $|\det U|=|\det V^*|=1$ for unitary matrices (from $U^*U=I$), leaving only $|\det\Sigma| = \prod\sigma_i$.
### Low-rank approximation via rank-one sums (p. 35, Theorem 5.7)
$A = \sum_{j=1}^r \sigma_ju_jv_j^*$ (eq. 5.3) - $A$ written as a sum of $r$ rank-one matrices, derived by splitting $\Sigma$ into $r$ rank-one diagonal pieces and substituting into $A=U\Sigma V^*$. The book is explicit that there are *many* ways to write any matrix as a sum of rank-one pieces (rows, columns, or Gaussian-elimination pieces all work) - what makes this particular decomposition special is Theorem 5.8. *Lecture, 2026-09-29:* the professor reconstructed this identical result live, calling the partial sums $A_\nu$ "a partial sum of the series" and connecting it directly to the rank-one matrix $uv^*$'s action: $Bv = uv^*v = u(v^*v) = u$ (using $\|v\|=1$), so $B$ maps $v\mapsto u$ and sends everything orthogonal to $v$ to zero - giving $\operatorname{range}(B)=\langle u\rangle$, $\operatorname{null}(B)=\langle v\rangle^\perp$, exactly the single-term version of Theorem 5.2.
### Best rank-$\nu$ approximation (Theorem 5.8, p. 35-36)
Define $A_\nu = \sum_{j=1}^\nu\sigma_ju_jv_j^*$ (eq. 5.4, with $\sigma_{\nu+1}:=0$ if $\nu=p$). Then $\|A-A_\nu\|_2 = \inf_{\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq\nu}\|A-B\|_2 = \sigma_{\nu+1}$ - **$A_\nu$ is the best possible rank-$\nu$ approximation to $A$ in the 2-norm, among *all* matrices of that rank, not just truncated-SVD candidates.** *Proof sketch (p. 36):* suppose some $B$ with $\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq\nu$ beats $A_\nu$'s error. Then $B$'s null space $W$ has dimension $n-\nu$, and for $w\in W$, $\|Aw\|=\|(A-B)w\|<\sigma_{\nu+1}\|w\|$. But the span of the first $\nu+1$ right singular vectors is a $(\nu+1)$-dimensional space where $\|Aw\|\geq\sigma_{\nu+1}\|w\|$ holds instead. Since $(n-\nu)+(\nu+1)>n$, these two subspaces must intersect nontrivially - a vector in both leads to a direct contradiction. *Geometric interpretation (p. 36):* the best rank-$\nu$ approximation to a hyperellipsoid is the one spanned by its $\nu$ longest axes - at each step, add the next-largest remaining axis; after $r$ steps, you've captured all of $A$. *Theorem 5.9 (p. 36, stated without proof)* gives the analogous Frobenius-norm result: $\|A-A_\nu\|_F = \sqrt{\sigma_{\nu+1}^2+\cdots+\sigma_r^2}$.
> [!NOTE] This is the Eckart-Young theorem, by name, in the research literature
> The book doesn't use that name here, but Theorem 5.8 is the result universally cited elsewhere as the Eckart-Young (or Eckart-Young-Mirsky) theorem. Worth knowing the name since it's how this result gets referenced outside this specific textbook.
### Why low-rank approximation matters computationally (lecture only, 2026-09-29)
The professor's own worked argument, not in the book's Lecture 5 text directly (the book defers computation/application detail to later lectures and exercises): storing $A_3$ (a rank-3 truncation) costs $3$ vectors of size $m$ ($u_i$'s) plus $3$ of size $n$ ($v_i$'s) plus $3$ scalars ($\sigma_i$'s) - roughly $3(m+n)$ numbers versus $mn$ for the full matrix. For a tall-skinny matrix where a small fraction of singular values carry almost all the "energy" (e.g. 80% near-zero), this is a large *storage* reduction. It's also a *computational* reduction: computing $A_3x$ directly as a matrix-vector product costs $O(mn)$, but using the factored form $A_3x = \sigma_1(v_1^*x)u_1 + \sigma_2(v_2^*x)u_2 + \sigma_3(v_3^*x)u_3$ costs only 3 inner products (each $O(n)$ or $O(m)$) plus a 3-term linear combination - the same "association trick" ($a(bc)$ instead of $(ab)c$) used throughout the book's operation-count arguments (see [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 7|Lecture 7]] and [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 8|Lecture 8]]'s Gram-Schmidt flop counts for the same pattern). *PCA connection, stated directly:* the principal components of PCA *are* the $u_i$ directions, "composite features" that are linear combinations of the original columns of $A$ (since $U$ comes from $AV\Sigma^{-1}$) - this is why PCA is, in the professor's words, "effectively just a singular value decomposition," and why reducing to the first few components is provably the *best* possible linear feature reduction, not merely a convenient heuristic.
### Computation of the SVD (p. 36-37)
Not addressed yet - the book is explicit that computing the SVD is "a fascinating subject" whose best methods are variants of eigenvalue algorithms, deferred to [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 31|Lecture 31]] (far beyond this course's current progress). The book flags that most of this lecture's theorems have direct computational consequences once an SVD is available: counting singular values above a tolerance is the best way to determine numerical rank (Theorem 5.1); Theorem 5.2 gives the most accurate orthonormal basis for range/null space (though QR is faster, if less accurate); Theorem 5.3 is the standard way to compute $\|A\|_2$; Theorems 5.8-5.9 are the standard methods for low-rank approximation itself.
## Worked Example
*The trace-based Frobenius norm derivation (p. 34, Theorem 5.3):* $\|A\|_F^2 := \operatorname{tr}(A^*A)$. Substituting $A=U\Sigma V^*$, $A^*A = V\Sigma^*U^*U\Sigma V^* = V\Sigma^2V^*$ (using $U^*U=I$, $\Sigma^*=\Sigma$ since diagonal). Trace is invariant under this unitary conjugation, so $\operatorname{tr}(A^*A)=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^2)=\sum\sigma_i^2$ - giving $\|A\|_F=\sqrt{\sum\sigma_i^2}$, depending on *every* singular value, unlike $\|A\|_2=\sigma_1$ which depends only on the largest.
*The rank-one matrix action, worked from lecture (2026-09-29):* for $B=uv^*$ with $\|u\|=\|v\|=1$, $Bv = uv^*v = u(v^*v) = u\cdot 1 = u$ (scalar association), and for any $w$ with $v^*w=0$, $Bw=uv^*w=u\cdot0=0$ - so $B$ maps its one special direction $v$ to $u$, and annihilates everything orthogonal to $v$. This single computation is the $\nu=1$ case of Theorems 5.1-5.2 made completely explicit.
