## Chapter Summary
==A projector is any matrix $P$ with $P^2=P$; geometrically, $Pv$ is the vector you get by casting $v$'s shadow onto range$(P)$, and once something is in that shadow, projecting it again changes nothing.== *Mechanism:* $P^2=P$ (idempotence, p. 41) forces every vector in range$(P)$ to be a fixed point of $P$, while $I-P$ is automatically a complementary projector satisfying range$(I-P)=\operatorname{null}(P)$ and null$(I-P)=\operatorname{range}(P)$ (p. 42, eq. 6.2-6.3) - a projector always splits $\mathbb{C}^m$ into two complementary subspaces. **Orthogonal projectors** (the special case where those two subspaces are mutually orthogonal, equivalently $P=P^*$) reduce to the strikingly simple formula $P=\hat Q\hat Q^*$ for any orthonormal basis $\hat Q$ of the target space (p. 45-46, eq. 6.6).
## Key Concepts
- **Projector**: square $P$ with $P^2=P$ (idempotent), p. 41.
- **Oblique projector**: a projector where range$(P)$ and null$(P)$ are *not* orthogonal (p. 41) - the general case.
- **Complementary projector**: $I-P$, always itself a projector, with range$(I-P)=\operatorname{null}(P)$ and null$(I-P)=\operatorname{range}(P)$ (p. 42, eq. 6.2-6.3).
- **Orthogonal projector**: a projector where range$(P)\perp$ null$(P)$; equivalently (Theorem 6.1, p. 43-44) $P=P^*$.
- **Projection with an orthonormal basis**: $P=\hat Q\hat Q^*$ (p. 45, eq. 6.6) for any $\hat Q$ with orthonormal columns - always a projector onto range$(\hat Q)$, regardless of how $\hat Q$ was obtained.
- **Rank-one projector**: $P_q = qq^*$ for unit $q$ (p. 46, eq. 6.8); its complement $P_{\perp q}=I-qq^*$ (eq. 6.9).
- **Generalized projector (arbitrary basis)**: $P=A(A^*A)^{-1}A^*$ (p. 47, eq. 6.13) - projects onto range$(A)$ for *any* full-rank $A$, orthonormal or not.
## Full Reading Notes
### Projectors and the shadow picture (p. 41)
$P^2=P$ (eq. 6.1, "idempotent"). The book's own physical metaphor: if light shines onto range$(P)$ from the right direction, $Pv$ is $v$'s shadow. If $v\in\operatorname{range}(P)$, $v=Px$ for some $x$, so $Pv=P^2x=Px=v$ - $v$ is already its own shadow, unchanged by projecting. For $v\neq Pv$, the "light direction" is $Pv-v$, and $P(Pv-v)=P^2v-Pv=0$ - that direction always lies in null$(P)$ (Figure 6.1, p. 42, an *oblique* projection example).
### Complementary projectors (p. 42)
$(I-P)^2 = I-2P+P^2 = I-P$ - so $I-P$ is automatically a projector too, called the **complementary projector**. range$(I-P)=\operatorname{null}(P)$ (eq. 6.2) and, symmetrically, null$(I-P)=\operatorname{range}(P)$ (eq. 6.3) - proved both directions (if $Pv=0$ then $(I-P)v=v$; conversely $(I-P)v=v-Pv\in\operatorname{null}(P)$ always). Also range$(P)\cap\operatorname{null}(P)=\{0\}$ (eq. 6.4) - any vector in both is forced to equal zero via $v=(I-P)v=0$. Conversely, given any two subspaces $S_1,S_2$ with $S_1\cap S_2=\{0\}$ and $S_1+S_2=\mathbb{C}^m$ ("complementary subspaces"), there's a *unique* projector $P$ onto $S_1$ along $S_2$ (p. 42-43) - this is the general existence statement behind "the projector onto $S_1$ along $S_2$."
### Orthogonal projectors: two equivalent definitions (p. 43-44)
An **orthogonal projector** projects onto $S_1$ along $S_2$ where $S_1\perp S_2$ (Figure 6.2, p. 43). *Warning stated directly in the book: "orthogonal projectors are not orthogonal matrices!"* (p. 43) - these are two unrelated uses of the word "orthogonal," worth keeping distinct. **Theorem 6.1:** $P$ is orthogonal $\iff P=P^*$ (hermitian). *Proof, both directions (p. 43-44):* if $P=P^*$, then for $Px\in S_1$ and $(I-P)y\in S_2$, $x^*P^*(I-P)y = x^*(P-P^2)y=0$ directly - orthogonality follows. The converse uses the SVD: given $P$ orthogonal with range$(P)$ of dimension $n$, build a unitary $Q$ whose first $n$ columns span range$(P)$ and remaining columns span null$(P)$; then $Q^*PQ=\Sigma$, a diagonal matrix with $n$ ones and the rest zeros (eq. 6.5) - this *is* an SVD of $P$, and from it $P^*=(Q\Sigma Q^*)^*=Q\Sigma Q^*=P$ directly.
### Projection with an orthonormal basis (p. 45-46)
Since an orthogonal projector's SVD has only 0s and 1s as singular values, dropping the "silent" zero columns of the full SVD gives the reduced form $P=\hat Q\hat Q^*$ (eq. 6.6) - strikingly simple. This holds for *any* $\hat Q$ with orthonormal columns, not just one that happens to come from an SVD: from the orthogonal-decomposition identity $v=r+\sum_i(q_iq_i^*)v$ (established in [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 2 & 3|Lecture 2 & 3]], eq. 2.7), the map $v\mapsto\sum_i(q_iq_i^*)v = \hat Q\hat Q^*v$ is exactly the orthogonal projector onto range$(\hat Q)$ (eq. 6.7). The complement $I-\hat Q\hat Q^*$ is also an orthogonal projector (hermitian, since $\hat Q\hat Q^*$ is), projecting onto the space orthogonal to range$(\hat Q)$. **Rank-one special case** (p. 46, eq. 6.8-6.9): for a single unit vector $q$, $P_q=qq^*$ isolates the component along $q$; its complement $P_{\perp q}=I-qq^*$ eliminates it. For an arbitrary nonzero $a$ (not necessarily unit length), the normalized forms are $P_a = aa^*/a^*a$ and $P_{\perp a}=I-aa^*/a^*a$ (eq. 6.10-6.11) - this exact pair is what [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 7|Lecture 7]]'s Gram-Schmidt derivation builds its successive projections out of.
### Projection with an arbitrary basis (p. 46-47)
What if the basis spanning the target subspace isn't orthonormal? Given full-rank $A$ with columns $\{a_j\}$ spanning the target subspace, the projection $y=Ax\in\operatorname{range}(A)$ of $v$ must satisfy $y-v\perp\operatorname{range}(A)$, i.e. $a_j^*(Ax-v)=0$ for every $j$, i.e. $A^*(Ax-v)=0$, i.e. $A^*Ax=A^*v$. Since $A$ has full rank, $A^*A$ is nonsingular (Exercise 6.3), so $x=(A^*A)^{-1}A^*v$ (eq. 6.12), giving $y=Ax=A(A^*A)^{-1}A^*v$ - so the **generalized projector onto range$(A)$** is $P=A(A^*A)^{-1}A^*$ (eq. 6.13). This is a direct multidimensional generalization of eq. 6.10; when $A=\hat Q$ (orthonormal), $(A^*A)^{-1}$ collapses to $I$ and this reduces exactly to eq. 6.6.
> [!IMPORTANT] This is the normal-equations formula for least squares
> The book doesn't name it here, but $A(A^*A)^{-1}A^*$ is exactly the projector buried inside the normal-equations solution to least squares, and $(A^*A)^{-1}A^*$ alone is the **Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse** restricted to full-rank, overdetermined $A$ - the professor named this connection explicitly in lecture (2026-10-01), ahead of where this course formally covers least squares in Part II.
### Lecture, 2026-09-29/10-01 — the projector-as-light-metaphor and the derivation path
The professor's own teaching order closely mirrors the book's, but built the orthogonal-projector SVD characterization *forward* from the geometric picture rather than stating Theorem 6.1 first: starting from "$P$ fixes directions already in range$(P)$" (the $P^2x=Px$ argument above, worked live almost identically to the book's p. 41), the professor derived that an orthogonal projector's SVD must have singular values only 0 or 1 - **1 for directions in range$(P)$ (fixed, not stretched), 0 for directions that get projected away** - directly matching eq. 6.5's $\Sigma$, then wrote $P=Q\Sigma Q^*$ with $U=V=Q$ collapsing to $P=QQ^*$, i.e. eq. 6.6, before the book's own $A(A^*A)^{-1}A^*$ generalization was derived live from the orthogonality condition $A^*(Ax-v)=0$ - step for step the same derivation as eq. 6.12-6.13 above. *A real moment of live self-correction, kept rather than smoothed over:* the professor briefly reversed which singular values (0 vs. 1) corresponded to fixed vs. projected-away directions mid-derivation, caught it, and corrected to the version matching the book exactly (1 = fixed/range, 0 = projected away/null space).
## Worked Example
The rank-one projector worked both ways: book's $P_a=aa^*/a^*a$ (eq. 6.10) applied to an arbitrary vector $v$ gives $P_av = a(a^*v)/(a^*a)$ - exactly the "component of $v$ in the direction of $a$" formula from a first linear algebra course (orthogonal decomposition $v = v_\parallel + v_\perp$), which the professor connected explicitly in lecture: $a_\parallel = (a^*b/b^*b)b$ is precisely $P_b a$ for the rank-one projector onto $b$, generalized here to arbitrary dimension via eq. 6.13 rather than just a single direction.
