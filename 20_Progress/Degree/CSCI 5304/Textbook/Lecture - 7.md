# Lecture 7 — QR Factorization
**Source:** Lloyd N. Trefethen and David Bau, III, *Numerical Linear Algebra*, 25th Anniversary Ed. (SIAM, 2022), Lecture 7, pp. 48-55.
**Read from:** `D:\_Anant\10_Areas\UMN\Classes\CSCI\CSCI 5304\Textbook & Resources\CSCI 5304 Textbook (MinerU full extraction).md`, lines 1793-2095.
**Course role:** Part II, QR Factorization and Least Squares. Taught 2026-10-01, directly after finishing [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 6|Lecture 6]] in the same session.
## Chapter Summary
==QR factorization constructs an orthonormal basis $q_1,q_2,\ldots$ that spans the same successive column spaces as $A$'s own columns $a_1,a_2,\ldots$ - and once you have it, solving $Ax=b$ collapses to a matrix-vector product plus back substitution instead of a hard elimination problem.== *Mechanism:* requiring $\langle q_1,\ldots,q_j\rangle = \langle a_1,\ldots,a_j\rangle$ for every $j$ (p. 48, eq. 7.1) forces an upper-triangular relationship between the $a$'s and $q$'s (eq. 7.2-7.3); $A=QR$ with $Q$ unitary and $R$ upper-triangular converts $Ax=b$ into $Rx=Q^*b$ (p. 54-55, eq. 7.13), a triangular system solved directly by back substitution.
## Key Concepts
- **Successive column spaces**: $\langle a_1\rangle \subseteq \langle a_1,a_2\rangle \subseteq \cdots$ (p. 48) - the nested spans QR factorization builds an orthonormal basis for, one column at a time.
- **Reduced QR factorization**: $A=\hat Q\hat R$ (p. 49, eq. 7.4), $\hat Q$ is $m\times n$ with orthonormal columns, $\hat R$ is $n\times n$ upper-triangular with (by convention) positive diagonal entries.
- **Full QR factorization**: $A=QR$ (p. 49-50), $Q$ extended to a full $m\times m$ unitary matrix by appending $m-n$ orthonormal columns, $R$ padded with zero rows.
- **Classical Gram-Schmidt** (Algorithm 7.1, p. 51): the direct algorithm implementing the successive-orthogonalization idea - unstable in floating point, but the simplest route to the existence proof.
- **Theorem 7.1** (existence, p. 51-52): every $A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}$ ($m\geq n$) has a full (hence reduced) QR factorization - proved by the Gram-Schmidt construction itself, with arbitrary vectors inserted wherever rank-deficiency makes $v_j=0$.
- **Theorem 7.2** (uniqueness, p. 52-53): a full-rank $A$'s reduced QR factorization is unique once $r_{jj}>0$ is fixed as a convention.
## Full Reading Notes
### Reduced QR factorization (p. 48-49)
For full-rank $A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}$ ($m\geq n$), the goal is an orthonormal sequence $q_1,q_2,\ldots$ with $\langle q_1,\ldots,q_j\rangle=\langle a_1,\ldots,a_j\rangle$ for every $j$ (eq. 7.1) - "from the observations of Lecture 1" (the column-space interpretation), this is equivalent to the matrix condition $A=\hat Q\hat R$ (eq. 7.2) with $\hat R$ upper-triangular, nonzero diagonal. Written out columnwise (eq. 7.3): $a_1=r_{11}q_1$, $a_2=r_{12}q_1+r_{22}q_2$, and so on - each $a_j$ is a combination of $q_1,\ldots,q_j$ only, never later $q$'s. This factorization, $A=\hat Q\hat R$ with $\hat Q$ $m\times n$ orthonormal-column and $\hat R$ $n\times n$ upper-triangular, is the **reduced QR factorization** (eq. 7.4).
### Full QR factorization (p. 49-50)
Appending $m-n$ more orthonormal columns extends $\hat Q$ to a full $m\times m$ unitary $Q$ - "analogous to the passage from the reduced to the full SVD described in Lecture 4" (p. 49), the identical hatted-notation convention [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 4|Lecture 4]] established. $\hat R$ is padded with $m-n$ rows of zeros to become $R$. The appended columns $q_j$ ($j>n$) are orthogonal to range$(A)$ - they form an orthonormal basis for range$(A)^\perp$, equivalently null$(A^*)$ (p. 50).
### Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization (p. 50-51)
Equations 7.3 directly suggest a construction: at step $j$, find a unit vector $q_j\in\langle a_1,\ldots,a_j\rangle$ orthogonal to $q_1,\ldots,q_{j-1}$. The needed orthogonalization is already known from [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 2 & 3|Lecture 2 & 3]]'s eq. 2.6: $v_j = a_j - \sum_{i<j}(q_i^*a_j)q_i$ (eq. 7.5), normalized by $\|v_j\|_2$ to get $q_j$. Rewriting eq. 7.3 to solve for each $q_j$ (eq. 7.6) identifies the coefficients directly: $r_{ij}=q_i^*a_j$ for $i\neq j$ (eq. 7.7), and $|r_{jj}| = \|a_j - \sum_{i<j}r_{ij}q_i\|_2$ for normalization (eq. 7.8) - the sign of $r_{jj}$ is a free convention, fixed here as positive. This is the **classical Gram-Schmidt iteration** (Algorithm 7.1): mathematically clean, but "numerically unstable because of rounding errors on a computer" (p. 51) - the book names it "classical" specifically to distinguish it from the stable **modified** version in [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 8|Lecture 8]].
### Existence and uniqueness (Theorems 7.1-7.2, p. 51-53)
*Existence:* the Gram-Schmidt algorithm itself is the proof - it can only fail if some $v_j=0$, which (for full-rank $A$) would mean $a_j\in\langle a_1,\ldots,a_{j-1}\rangle$, contradicting full rank. If $A$ *isn't* full rank, pick $q_j$ arbitrarily (any unit vector orthogonal to the previous $q$'s) whenever $v_j=0$, and continue - "the issues discussed in the last two paragraphs came up already in Lecture 4, in our discussion of the SVD" (p. 52), the book's own explicit cross-reference to the identical rank-deficient-SVD construction. *Uniqueness (Theorem 7.2):* for full-rank $A$, the reduced QR factorization with $r_{jj}>0$ is unique - any other factorization only differs by multiplying $\hat Q$'s $i$-th column and $\hat R$'s $i$-th row by a unit-modulus scalar $z$, and fixing $r_{jj}>0$ removes that one remaining degree of freedom.
### When vectors become continuous functions (p. 53-54)
A genuinely surprising extension: replace $\mathbb{C}^m$ with $L^2[-1,1]$ (square-integrable functions), inner product $(f,g)=\int_{-1}^1\bar f(x)g(x)\,dx$ (eq. 7.9). The "matrix" $A=[1\mid x\mid x^2\mid\cdots\mid x^{n-1}]$ (eq. 7.10) - its columns are monomials, a continuous analogue of the Vandermonde matrix from Lecture 1. Its "QR factorization" produces the **Legendre polynomials** $P_0=1$, $P_1=x$, $P_2=\frac32x^2-\frac12$, $P_3=\frac52x^3-\frac32x$ (eq. 7.11) - orthogonal (not just a spanning set like the monomials), which is why they're preferred for spectral methods solving PDEs numerically (p. 54). The projection matrix $\hat Q\hat Q^*$ becomes an *integral operator* in this setting (eq. 7.12) - the same formula as [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 6|Lecture 6]]'s eq. 6.6, just acting on functions instead of finite vectors.
### Solving $Ax=b$ by QR factorization (p. 54-55)
For nonsingular square $A=QR$: $QRx=b \iff Rx=Q^*b$ (eq. 7.13). The right side is easy to compute given $Q$; the left side is an upper-triangular system, solved directly by back substitution. **Algorithm: (1) compute $A=QR$; (2) compute $y=Q^*b$; (3) solve $Rx=y$ by back substitution.** The book states directly that this is "an excellent method" for solving linear systems - proved stable in Lecture 16 - but *not* the standard one in practice, since Gaussian elimination needs only half as many flops (p. 55).
### Lecture, 2026-10-01 — the "what and why" of QR, and the live Gram-Schmidt derivation
The professor opened with the book's own quoted framing (the successive-column-space idea) almost verbatim, then worked the exact same reduced/full distinction, with the identical visual convention: $Q=[\hat Q \mid Q_2]$, $R=\begin{bmatrix}\hat R\\0\end{bmatrix}$. **On "why QR":** the professor gave the identical linear-solve motivation as the book's p. 54-55 (QR converts $Ax=b$ into a unitary step plus a triangular back-substitution step), explicitly previewing that Gaussian elimination's instability (not yet covered in this course) is exactly why the QR-based route matters once stability is studied - "step one can be done in a way that minimizes error... the whole thing will be stable... because it's composed of backwards stable algorithms," a direct forward-link to Part III. **The live Gram-Schmidt derivation** matched eq. 7.3-7.8 column by column: $a_1=r_{11}q_1 \Rightarrow r_{11}=\|a_1\|_2$; $a_2=r_{12}q_1+r_{22}q_2 \Rightarrow r_{12}=q_1^*a_2$ (derived from the orthogonality requirement $q_1^*q_2=0$, not just asserted), $r_{22}=\|a_2-r_{12}q_1\|_2$ - and the professor explicitly reframed the subtraction $a_2-(q_1^*a_2)q_1$ as $(I-q_1q_1^*)a_2$, i.e. [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 6|Lecture 6]]'s complementary rank-one projector $P_{\perp q_1}$ applied directly - the same connection [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 8|Lecture 8]]'s book text makes formally via eq. 8.1-8.3.
## Worked Example
The book's own continuous QR example (p. 53-54): factoring $A=[1\mid x\mid x^2\mid x^3]$ over $L^2[-1,1]$ produces the first four Legendre polynomials $1, x, \frac32x^2-\frac12, \frac52x^3-\frac32x$ as the "columns" of $Q$ - a concrete demonstration that Gram-Schmidt's logic applies to *any* inner-product space, not just $\mathbb{C}^m$, and that the resulting orthogonal basis (unlike the original monomial basis) is "far better suited for certain computations," specifically the spectral methods used for numerically solving PDEs.
