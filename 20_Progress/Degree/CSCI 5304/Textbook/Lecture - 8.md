## Chapter Summary
==Classical and modified Gram-Schmidt compute mathematically identical results via different sequences of floating-point operations - and that sequencing difference alone is the entire reason one is numerically stable and the other isn't.== *Mechanism:* classical Gram-Schmidt computes each $v_j$ via one combined projection $P_ja_j$ (p. 56, eq. 8.4); modified Gram-Schmidt computes the same $v_j$ via $j-1$ successive rank-$(m-1)$ projections $P_{\perp q_{j-1}}\cdots P_{\perp q_1}a_j$ (p. 56-57, eq. 8.5-8.7) - algebraically equal, but accumulating rounding error differently, because each step immediately uses the most up-to-date, already-orthogonalized intermediate vector rather than the original $a_j$.
## Key Concepts
- **Gram-Schmidt projections**: $q_j = P_ja_j/\|P_ja_j\|$ (p. 56, eq. 8.1), where $P_j$ is the rank-$(m-(j-1))$ orthogonal projector onto the space orthogonal to $\langle q_1,\ldots,q_{j-1}\rangle$, i.e. $P_j = I-\hat Q_{j-1}\hat Q_{j-1}^*$ (eq. 8.2-8.3).
- **Modified Gram-Schmidt**: computes $v_j$ via a *sequence* of $j-1$ rank-$(m-1)$ projections $P_{\perp q_i}$ applied one at a time (eq. 8.6-8.7), rather than one combined rank-$(m-j+1)$ projection $P_j$ - mathematically identical to classical GS, numerically more stable.
- **Flop**: one addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, or square root (p. 58) - the book's unit for operation counts.
- **Theorem 8.1** (p. 58): both classical and modified Gram-Schmidt require $\sim 2mn^2$ flops for an $m\times n$ QR factorization.
- **Triangular orthogonalization**: each outer step of modified Gram-Schmidt is a right-multiplication by an upper-triangular matrix $R_i$ (p. 61-62, eq. 8.10); the full process is $A\underbrace{R_1R_2\cdots R_n}_{\hat R^{-1}} = \hat Q$ - the structural contrast to [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 10|Lecture 10]]'s Householder "orthogonal triangularization."
## Full Reading Notes
### Gram-Schmidt projections, reframed (p. 56)
Lecture 7's equations 7.6-7.8 are rewritten here using projectors directly: $q_j = P_ja_j/\|P_ja_j\|$ (eq. 8.1), where $P_j$ is the $m\times m$ rank-$(m-(j-1))$ orthogonal projector onto the orthogonal complement of $\langle q_1,\ldots,q_{j-1}\rangle$ (with $P_1=I$). Explicitly, $P_j = I - \hat Q_{j-1}\hat Q_{j-1}^*$ (eq. 8.2-8.3), using [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 6|Lecture 6]]'s projection-with-an-orthonormal-basis formula directly.
### Modified Gram-Schmidt algorithm (p. 57-58)
Classical GS (Algorithm 7.1) computes $v_j=P_ja_j$ as *one* combined rank-$(m-j+1)$ projection (eq. 8.4). The book shows $P_j = P_{\perp q_{j-1}}\cdots P_{\perp q_2}P_{\perp q_1}$ (eq. 8.5) - a product of $j-1$ rank-$(m-1)$ projectors, each removing one direction at a time. **Modified Gram-Schmidt computes $v_j$ by applying this product one factor at a time** (eq. 8.6-8.7): $v_j^{(1)}=a_j$, $v_j^{(2)}=v_j^{(1)}-q_1q_1^*v_j^{(1)}$, $v_j^{(3)}=v_j^{(2)}-q_2q_2^*v_j^{(2)}$, and so on. *Mathematically these are identical to classical GS* - but "in finite precision computer arithmetic... (8.7) introduces smaller errors than (8.4)" (p. 58), because each successive subtraction operates on an already-partially-cleaned vector rather than repeatedly re-projecting the raw $a_j$. **Algorithm 8.1 (Modified Gram-Schmidt):** for $i=1$ to $n$: $v_i=a_i$; then for $i=1$ to $n$: $r_{ii}=\|v_i\|$, $q_i=v_i/r_{ii}$, and for $j=i+1$ to $n$: $r_{ij}=q_i^*v_j$, $v_j=v_j-r_{ij}q_i$ - the key structural difference from classical GS is that each newly computed $q_i$ is immediately applied to *all remaining* $v_j$'s, rather than waiting until column $j$'s own turn.
### Operation count (Theorem 8.1, p. 58-59)
Both Algorithms 7.1 and 8.1 require $\sim 2mn^2$ flops (one flop = one add/subtract/multiply/divide/sqrt, p. 58) - the book explicitly discards lower-order terms, standard practice "unless $m$ and $n$ are small." *Derivation (p. 59):* the inner loop's $r_{ij}=q_i^*v_j$ costs $\sim m$ flops (inner product), and $v_j=v_j-r_{ij}q_i$ costs $\sim m$ more - $\sim 4m$ flops per inner iteration (eq. 8.8), summed over $\sum_i\sum_{j>i} 4m \sim 2mn^2$.
### Counting operations geometrically (p. 59-60)
An alternative, purely visual derivation: picture the algorithm's work as a solid shrinking from an $m\times n$ rectangle down to a single column, converging (as $m,n\to\infty$) to a right triangular prism of volume $mn^2/2$; multiplying by 4 flops per unit volume reproduces $\sim 2mn^2$ (eq. 8.9) exactly. The book states this geometric-counting habit is used throughout the rest of the book "as a reminder of the structure of an algorithm," not just as a counting shortcut (p. 60) - the identical geometric-volume technique [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 10|Lecture 10]] reuses for Householder's own flop count.
### Gram-Schmidt as triangular orthogonalization (p. 61-62)
Each outer step of modified GS is a right-multiplication by an upper-triangular matrix $R_i$: the first step multiplies column 1 by $1/r_{11}$ and subtracts multiples of it from later columns, equivalent to right-multiplying by a specific triangular $R_1$; step $i$ similarly corresponds to a triangular $R_i$ (p. 61-62). Chaining all $n$ steps: $A\underbrace{R_1R_2\cdots R_n}_{\hat R^{-1}} = \hat Q$ (eq. 8.10) - **Gram-Schmidt is a method of *triangular orthogonalization***: triangular operations applied on the *right* reduce $A$ to orthonormal columns. The book flags this as the structural opposite of [[20_Progress/Degree/CSCI 5304/Textbook/Lecture - 10|Lecture 10]]'s Householder method ("orthogonal triangularization": unitary operations on the *left* reduce $A$ to upper-triangular form) and notes a close resemblance to the structure of Gaussian elimination, covered much later (Lecture 20).

## Worked Example
The commutativity argument the professor actually worked through live, matching the book's own eq. 8.5 structure: for orthonormal $q_1,q_2$, $(I-q_2q_2^*)(I-q_1q_1^*) = I - q_1q_1^* - q_2q_2^* + q_2q_2^*q_1q_1^*$, and the cross term $q_2(q_2^*q_1)q_1^* = 0$ because $q_2^*q_1=0$ (orthogonality) - leaving $I-q_1q_1^*-q_2q_2^*$, symmetric in $q_1,q_2$, confirming the two projectors can be applied in either order without changing the result. This is exactly why modified Gram-Schmidt is free to apply each new $(I-q_iq_i^*)$ to all remaining columns immediately, instead of waiting for each column's own turn in sequence.
