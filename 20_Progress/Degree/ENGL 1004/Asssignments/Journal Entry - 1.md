Draft to copy by hand:

Tuesday’s lecture on Ulysses stuck with me while reading into the state-law session. Judge Woolsey rejected the question of whether an isolated passage from the book could corrupt the most vulnerable reader, as the Hicklin test would have had him do. He asked instead what the book as a whole was about; this is the same distinction that guided my analysis while skimming through the first six chapters of Huckleberry Finn this week.

Chapter six finds Pap hissing about a free black professor in Ohio who can vote, the sort of citizen the racist uses the racial slur Twain otherwise reserves for Jim throughout the novel. Taken freely from the page, as Utah’s proposed HB29 would have it, this is precisely the sort of passage that would see a book banned from any school district in the state. The group investigating HB29 found that three districts together could black out an entire book by combing through it for such passages, which would then be subject to a ban across the state without needing to be reviewed in context. Read as intended by the author, however, Huck’s father is better understood as a dissolute fool who deserved the beating he got from Huck and Jim for trying to steal his son; the joke lands squarely on his drunken, abusive behavior rather than on any supposed slur.

This is the sort of context that an isolated-passage law like Utah’s proposed HB29 or California’s AB1168 fail to consider, in favor of letting chapters be judged by isolated paragraphs and even words. What troubles me more are Minnesota’s very own laws pertaining to book bans, however. Subdivision 3 of section 134.51, the “book banning prohibited” statute I was under the impression protected Minnesota’s classrooms from bans like those seen in Utah and now Alabama, contains language nearly identical to what I’ve since found in Florida’s infamous “don’t say gay” bill. Both use the same phrasing of removal being inappropriate due to “legitimate pedagogical concerns… the appropriateness of potentially sensitive topics.” One would be banned outright from being taught, the other from being read in any context a teacher saw fit. All it takes is a change in who controls the law and what they’re able to prohibit in the first place. The phrase I found most troubling as a potential slur, however, was not in the law itself but rather in the lesson I took from it. I understood going into this exercise that a slur used in the book could see it banned by those who believed it was one. What surprised me more, however, was learning that the law protecting such books could do the opposite if put in the wrong hands.
