## What This Course Actually Covers
A course about book banning, taught by reading the actual banned/challenged books alongside real First Amendment case law and state legislation. Not literary analysis in the abstract - the course culminates in a **live school-board simulation** where every student role-plays a real stakeholder (school board member, a parent aligned with Moms for Liberty, a community member opposed to any banning, a local reporter, a lawyer) arguing over three specific challenged titles: *And Tango Makes Three* (elementary read-aloud), *Melissa* (5th/6th grade classroom libraries), and *The Bluest Eye* (high school curriculum).

## Journal Entries
12 entries across the semester, handwritten, graded on a 0–2 scale per entry: **2** = well-considered, genuinely explores an issue the text raises; **1** = insufficiently considered, or late by up to a week; **0** = not submitted, or more than a week late. Early entries (1–3) are written "in your own voice" - real reactions with concrete textual evidence, not just feelings. Entries 4–6 and beyond shift to writing *as your assigned simulation character* - reasoning the way that character would, not as yourself.
## Role Creation & Simulation
Roles get assigned after a preference questionnaire, then fleshed out in class with a background and personal objectives - once set, **you're expected to stick to them** through the simulation. The simulation itself runs across three class sessions (10/8, 10/13, 10/20) as a school-board meeting on the three challenged titles, followed by a debrief on media reporting (10/22). Grading is based on how well you hold your objectives and how engaged you are - only students assigned to report on the proceedings may use a computer or electronic note-taking device during it.
## Class Participation
Explicitly **not the same as attendance** - graded on engagement and intellectual risk-taking. Being physically present but visibly disengaged scores a 0 for that day, same as an absence would for attendance purposes.
## Attendance
Mandatory, with an escalating penalty structure: missing more than **one cumulative week** unexcused lowers your grade; missing **three cumulative weeks** unexcused fails the course outright. Coming to class unprepared can get you asked to leave, counted as an unexcused absence.
## Electronic Devices
No use in class except when specifically invited. First violation gets a verbal warning; the **second violation is an automatic 0 for that day's participation grade** - stated as a deliberate policy, not an idle threat, with the instructor citing both anecdotal and research-based effects on retention.
## Final Exam
One in-class exam on the last scheduled day (**2026-12-15**) - passage identification from the readings, then discussion connecting the passage to the larger work and to the semester's discussions.
## AI Policy
> [!DANGER] The strictest AI ban of any Fall'26 course
> "Generative AI is NOT allowed for this class," stated in full caps in the syllabus itself - not a soft preference. The instructor's own reasoning: AI "spits out the most obvious takes only," including incorrect ones, simplifies and smooths over subtlety, and blocks the actual skill the course is teaching (working through a difficult text to your own opinion). The syllabus states a specific detection method - the instructor has tested the actual readings/assignments against AI models and predicts what AI-assisted work looks like: mediocre (low-B) class contributions, poor exam performance, a shallow simulation role, or a mismatch between real-time class performance and written work quality. **If AI use is suspected, the consequence is a standing requirement to discuss readings weekly and write journal entries during the instructor's office hours** - not a one-time penalty, an ongoing supervision arrangement. Separately, **plagiarism explicitly includes AI-generated submissions** and can fail the assignment or the course outright.
