## Submission record
### Profile of a Successful Entrepreneur: Justin Kittredge and ISlide, Inc.
Justin Kittredge founded ISlide, Inc. in January 2013 out of an office in Wellesley, Massachusetts, making customizable athletic sandals ("slides"). Before that, he spent thirteen years in footwear: four years in Reebok sales and marketing, two years as a product manager at Atsco Footwear, then back to Reebok as Director of Product Development for Performance Basketball, managing shoe design, running field tests, and working directly with NBA players and factories in China. When Reebok eliminated his division in December 2012, he had a signed noncompete but thirteen years of manufacturing relationships and product-development knowledge. He used the layoff as the trigger, funded ISlide with his own savings, and launched six months later.

I picked Kittredge over more famous names I considered, Sara Blakely and Airbnb's founders, because his path argues against an assumption I carry into this course without noticing. I am a computer science major finishing a semester built around building things from scratch — a portfolio site, a market-research tool, an agentic automation pipeline — aimed at a software or AI engineering internship next summer. That framing makes it easy to think a founder needs one clever, original idea. Kittredge did not have one. He spent thirteen years accumulating narrow, specific expertise — sourcing contacts in China, the footwear product-development cycle, athlete relationships — before building anything of his own, matching the exact pattern Chapter 2 names: eighty percent of Inc. 500 founders start in or near their current industry. That reframes the semester. The technical depth I am building right now is not separate from a future venture; on Kittredge's evidence, it is the precondition for one.

Kittredge moved fast because that expertise was already in place. Within six months of the layoff he designed the slides, lined up a Chinese manufacturer who could hit his quality bar, and found a local printer for customization, bringing printing in-house within a year. By month eleven he had five employees and an intern pipeline pulling unpaid students from Babson, Harvard, and Boston College, stretching limited capital. Revenue moved from roughly $50,000 in 2013 to a projected $250,000-$350,000 in 2014. He built the brand almost entirely on athlete relationships and social media — custom slides for Celtics players and NBA draft picks, a "Boston Strong" line tied to a real cause — instead of paid advertising, and pushed distribution from individual buyers toward bulk team sales through channels like Dick's Sporting Goods.

The lesson is not "hustle harder." The six-month launch timeline was purchased over thirteen years, not compressed by willpower. Kittredge also never took a salary and ran the company on unpaid interns and a $4.50-per-square-foot warehouse office, spending real money only on the one thing that actually differentiated the product: an in-house printer. Applied to where I am now, that argues for treating this semester's technical work as the expertise-building phase itself, and for being just as deliberate later about which few things are worth real capital.

*Source: Stoddard, D., & Balachandra, L. (2016). Case: ISlide, Inc. In A. Zacharakis, W. D. Bygrave, & A. C. Corbett,* Entrepreneurship *(5th ed., pp. 95-101). Wiley.*
