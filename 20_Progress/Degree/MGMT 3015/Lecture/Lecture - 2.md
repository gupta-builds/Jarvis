## Key ideas (short)
- The **entrepreneurial-mindset quiz** isn't a personality test with a pass/fail line — it's a self-check against behaviors (selling something before, tolerating imperfection, noticing "there should be a better way") rather than traits.
- Entrepreneurs live inside **paradoxes**, not resolved contradictions: risk-taker *and* risk-averse, overconfident *and* humble enough to pivot, independent *and* reliant on others. The paradox framing matters because it rules out "just be more risk-tolerant" as useful advice.
- The **discovery → evaluation → exploitation → context** diagram reappears from Session 1, now framing the "why are some people more entrepreneurial" question — differences trace to traits, resources, culture, cognitive bias, and prior experience, not one dominant factor.
- A **supportive environment** is built from networks (family, role models, advisors, colleagues), geography, and employer — entrepreneurship is not a solo-personality outcome.

## Lecture
### 1. Agenda
Three stated topics: Entrepreneurial Attributes, Entrepreneurial Environment, Entrepreneurial Process — tagged directly to Chapters 1 & 2.
### 2. Entrepreneurial-mindset self-quiz
Ten true/untrue statements split across two slides (see Examples above for the sharpest five), followed by a scoring key: 8-10 true = "born hustler," 5-7 true = "strong entrepreneurial potential, keep exploring," 0-4 true = "entrepreneurship is a skill anyone can grow" — a deliberately non-gatekeeping framing (nobody is told they can't be entrepreneurial).
### 3. "Fix My Day" — real-world problem solving
Three-part live exercise in groups of 3: (a) individually jot down "one small but annoying problem you faced in the last 24-48 hours"; (b) 10-minute group brainstorm choosing one problem and generating 2-3 rough solutions, explicitly encouraged to be wild rather than realistic; (c) 5-minute rapid pitch per group answering what's the problem, what's the solution, who would use it. This is the same discovery-stage move the course will repeat in Session 4's ideation exercises and Session 5's opportunity evaluation.
### 4. The process diagram, reused
Opportunity Discovery → Opportunity Evaluation → Opportunity Exploitation, inside Context — shown again verbatim from Session 1, now used as the frame for the next section's question.
### 5. The five paradoxes of entrepreneurs
See Examples above for the full list. Framing line: entrepreneurs hold both sides of each tension at once — this is presented as the *normal* condition, not a flaw to train out.
### 6. Why are some people more entrepreneurial than others? Nature vs. nurture
Direct question posed to the class, then answered with five candidate factors (Slide 13): personality traits (risk tolerance, openness to experience), access to resources (financial capital, networks), cultural and social influences, cognitive biases (optimism, overconfidence), and prior experience (exposure to entrepreneurship, industry knowledge). No single factor is privileged — the slide lists all five as co-explanations.
### 7. What makes a supportive entrepreneurial environment?
Answered with: networks (parents/family, role models, advisors, colleagues), geography, and employer — labeled "Sociological and Environmental Factors" on the slide.
### 8. Serial entrepreneurs
Two open questions posed with no slide answer given: "Why do they do it?" and "What do they learn from experience?" — left as class-discussion prompts, not resolved in the deck itself.

## Lecture-to-textbook synthesis
==Entrepreneurs operate inside five real paradoxes at once — risk-taking and risk-averse, overconfident and humble, independent and reliant on others — and this tension, not a fixed personality profile, is what nature-vs-nurture research and the course's own environment factors are trying to explain.==
*Mechanism:* If entrepreneurship were a single stable trait, the nature/nurture question would have a clean answer. Instead the slide gives five co-existing factors (traits, resources, culture, cognitive bias, prior experience) precisely because the paradoxes mean no one trait dominates — someone can be risk-tolerant by personality but risk-averse by environment (limited resources), and the outcome depends on which factor is load-bearing in their specific case.
- Lecture example/scenario: the "Fix My Day" exercise puts the discovery stage into practice under exactly this tension — groups are told to "encourage wild ideas, the goal is to think creatively, not realistically (yet)," then immediately forced into a realistic 1-minute pitch, enacting the dreamer-vs-realist paradox live.
- Textbook connection: [[20_Progress/Degree/_Courses/Technical Interview/CSCI 4041/Textbook/Chapter - 1 & 2]] — Chapter 2's "Ingredients for a Successful New Business" is the book's structured answer to the same "why some succeed" question the nature/nurture slide poses informally.
