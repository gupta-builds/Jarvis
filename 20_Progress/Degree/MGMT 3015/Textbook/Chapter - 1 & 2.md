# Chapter - 1 & 2 — The Power of Entrepreneurship / The Entrepreneurial Process

## Chapter 1: The Power of Entrepreneurship

*Context:* Chapter 1 opening introduces the global impact of new ventures, opening with Airbnb founders Nathan Blecharczyk, Brian Chesky, and Joe Gebbia (p. 1-2). Hundreds of new businesses are born every hour of every working day in the United States (p. 2). New and emerging businesses create half of all new private-sector jobs in the U.S. (2018 data) and played a central role in economic recovery; a 2015 Small Business Administration (SBA) report indicates entrepreneurs created 7 of 11 million new jobs following the 2008 recession (p. 2).

*Schumpeterian Foundation:* Moravian-born economist Joseph Schumpeter (writing in Vienna in the early 20th century) defined an entrepreneur as ==a person who destroys the existing economic order by introducing new products and services, by introducing new methods of production, by creating new forms of organization, or by exploiting new raw materials== (p. 2). Schumpeter explained that economic growth and rising living standards stem from entrepreneurial innovation. Following the Industrial Revolution in England (~1760), standard of living jumped sevenfold across 16 industrialized nations between 1870 and 1979 (p. 2). Schumpeterian "gales of creative destruction" disrupt established markets (e.g., Airbnb in hospitality, Uber in taxis), though most startups enter existing markets incrementally (p. 2).

*Textbook Definitions:* The textbook adopts a broader definition: an **entrepreneur** is a person who perceives an opportunity and creates an organization to pursue it (p. 3). The **entrepreneurial process** encompasses all functions, activities, and actions associated with perceiving opportunities and creating organizations to pursue them (p. 3).

*Case Sidebar — The Changing Economy:*
- *General Electric (GE):* Founded in Schenectady, NY in 1892, GE grew into a dominant conglomerate (aircraft engines, locomotives, oil/gas, healthcare, finance). Ranked 18th in Fortune 500 gross revenue in 2018 (4th largest and 14th most profitable a few years prior). Stock fell from $20–$30 (late 1990s–mid-2017) to under $9 in early 2019 due to market shifts, bad investments, and entrepreneurial gales of destruction (p. 2-3).
- *Walmart vs. Amazon:* Sam Walton founded Walmart in 1962, expanding sales toward $500 billion as the world's largest retailer. Amazon started as an online bookseller and entered groceries by acquiring Whole Foods Market in 2017, becoming the market leader in online groceries and competing directly with Walmart (p. 3).

### Entrepreneurship and Small Business in the United States
*Job Creation Statistics:* Between 2000 and 2017, U.S. small businesses created 8.4 million net new jobs compared to 4.4 million by large businesses (p. 3). Between 2015 and 2017, small firms outpaced large firms in job growth across high-tech sectors, including software, pharmaceuticals, scientific R&D, and computer systems design (p. 3). Small firms hire 43% of all U.S. high-tech workers and produce approximately 16 times more patents per employee than large firms; these patents are twice as likely to be among the top 1% most cited (p. 3).
*Structural Distribution:* Over one-third of the 30 million U.S. small businesses are in professional, technical, and related services (p. 3). The majority are non-employer firms run by single proprietors. Approximately 17% of firms employ 1 to 20 people, and over 500,000 firms employ between 20 and 499 people (p. 3). Health care, hospitality/food services, and retail are leading small business employers (p. 3).
*Business Survival Rates:* Alfred Nucci's U.S. Census Bureau study established 10-year establishment survival benchmarks:
- *1-Year Survival:* 81% overall (79% for independent startups) (p. 4).
- *2-Year Survival:* 65% (p. 4).
- *5-Year Survival:* 40% (p. 4).
- *10-Year Survival:* 25% (p. 4).
- *Determinants:* Survival probability increases with firm age and size; industry variation has a weaker effect than age and size (p. 4).
*Owner Satisfaction & Income:* Median small business owner income equals wage/salary earners, but owner income distribution is much broader (p. 4). A disposal study found only 17% of owners reported their business raised their standard of living, while 6% reported a decline, and most saw little financial lifestyle change (p. 4). However, survey data shows small business owners report higher life satisfaction ("close to ideal") than non-entrepreneurs (p. 4).
*Historical Shift in Policy:* Net business formation was flat through the 1950s–1960s and began accelerating around 1970 (p. 4). Journalist Norman Macrae published "The Coming Entrepreneurial Revolution" in *The Economist* (Dec 1976), arguing big business dominance was ending (p. 4). Economist David Birch published *Job Creation in America* (1978), demonstrating that small firms generate most new jobs, shifting policymaker focus away from corporate conglomerates (p. 4). In 1953, GM President Charles Erwin Wilson claimed before Senate hearings, "What's good for General Motors is good for the country" (GM employed >600,000 workers in the 1970s; creative destruction reduced GM employment below 200,000 today) (p. 4).

### Entrepreneurial Revolution
*Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) Realignment:* On November 1, 1999, Chevron, Goodyear Tire & Rubber, Sears Roebuck, and Union Carbide were replaced in the DJIA by Intel, Microsoft, Home Depot, and SBC Communications (p. 4-5). Intel and Microsoft were the first NASDAQ-traded stocks added to the DJIA (p. 4). SBC originated from the Department of Justice antitrust breakup of AT&T's monopoly ("Baby Bells") (p. 5).
*Employee Career Aspirations:* MarketWatch survey data highlights corporate workplace shifts:
- *General Intent:* 39% of U.S. employees aspire to own a business someday (p. 4).
- *By Age Cohort:* >50% of non-owner employees in their 20s want to start a business; 50% in their 30s want to leave their jobs to start one; 35% in their 40s share this goal (p. 4).
*Founding Profiles:*
- *Intel:* Founded in Silicon Valley by Gordon Moore (**Moore's Law**) and Robert Noyce (co-inventor of the integrated circuit), funded by venture capitalist Arthur Rock (p. 5). Moore and Noyce previously worked at Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory (Mountain View, 1956) under transistor co-inventor William Shockley, left in 1957 to found Fairchild Semiconductor (which introduced the first commercial integrated circuit in 1961), and launched Intel in 1968 (p. 5). Bill Gates noted: "When I was 19, I caught sight of the future..." (p. 5).
- *Home Depot:* Founded in 1979 by Bernie Marcus and Arthur Blank (p. 5-6). It reached $30B, $40B, $50B, $60B, and $70B annual revenue milestones faster than any retailer in history, joining the DJIA at age 20 (compared to Walmart joining at age 35 to replace F.W. Woolworth) (p. 6). Home Depot and Walmart combined employ 2.7 million workers (p. 6).
*Macroeconomic Sectoral Transformation:*
- *1900:* ~50% of U.S. workers were in agriculture/domestic service; by 2000, that figure fell to ~4% due to agricultural productivity gains and labor-saving household appliances (vacuums, electric ranges, washers/dryers, dishwashers, automobiles, microwaves) (p. 6).
- *Manufacturing:* Grew from 19% of workforce in 1900 to 27% in 1950 (p. 6).
- *Current Distribution:* ~12% in goods-producing sectors, 80% in service-providing sectors, and >50% in knowledge-based jobs (p. 6).
- *DJIA Composition Evolution:* 1896 (12 commodity/ag firms), 1928 (30 industrial/manufacturing firms), 2019 (knowledge-based, telecom, financial services firms) (p. 6-7).
*Non-DJIA Entrepreneurial Pioneers:*
- *FedEx:* Fred Smith conceptualized nationwide overnight package delivery in a Yale undergraduate term paper (receiving a "C" grade) (p. 6-7). Founded Federal Express in 1971, starting operations at Memphis International Airport in 1973 (p. 6-7). Smith led lobbying for air cargo deregulation (enacted 1977); FedEx ships 15 million packages daily across 220+ countries/territories, connecting >99% of global GDP (p. 6-7).
- *Genentech & Biotechnology:* Robert Swanson (age 27) partnered with UCSF Professor Herbert Boyer (co-inventor of recombinant DNA patents) in 1976 to found Genentech (**genetic engineering technology**) (p. 8). Announced synthetic somatostatin seven months later (p. 8). Genentech's October 1980 IPO launched the commercial biotech industry (today >20,000 biotech firms globally, generating $140B revenue among 700 U.S. public firms) (p. 8).
- *Apple Computer:* Steve Jobs and Stephen Wozniak launched Apple in 1975–1976 (p. 8). The Byte Shop (first full-time computer store, opened Dec 1975) ordered 25 Apple I kits; owner requested fully cased units, sparking retail microcomputer demand (p. 8). Apple went public in December 1980 (p. 8).
- *VisiCalc & Software:* Dan Bricklin (Harvard MBA student, 1978) and Bob Frankston created VisiCalc for Apple II (Software Arts, May 1979), driving personal computer adoption (p. 8). Mitch Kapor later developed Lotus 1-2-3 for the IBM PC (p. 8).
- *Hard Drives & Storage:* Al Shugart (Shugart Associates, Seagate) pioneered hard drive miniaturization (p. 8). Dick Eagan and Roger Marino founded EMC Corporation in 1979 (selling computer furniture, then memory; achieved the highest single-decade stock return on the NYSE in the 1990s; later merged into Dell-EMC) (p. 8-9).
*Web Convergence:* Tim Berners-Lee created Hypertext Markup Language (**HTML**) and the World Wide Web at CERN in 1989 (p. 9-10). Three distinct revolutions converged: digital technology, information technology (IT), and entrepreneurship (p. 10). By 1992, the Internet had 1 million hosts, computers were 1,000 million times faster, and network bandwidth was 20 million times greater than 20 years earlier (p. 10). Marc Andreessen released Mosaic browser alpha at NCSA (Feb 1993); by Dec 1994, Web usage grew 1%/day (doubling under 10 weeks), reaching 4 billion users by 2018 (~50% world population) (p. 10).

### Entrepreneurship Revolution Strikes Gold
*Netscape Commercialization:* Marc Andreessen and Jim Clark incorporated Mosaic Communications (renamed Netscape Communications) in 1994 with $6M personal capital from Clark and $6M venture capital (p. 10). Programmers worked 18-hour days, 7 days a week (sometimes 48 hours straight) (p. 10). Released Netscape Navigator in late 1994, capturing 75% of the browser market in four months (p. 10). Netscape's August 1995 IPO (16 months post-founding) made Jim Clark the first Internet billionaire; CEO Jim Barksdale amassed wealth within eight months, igniting the dot-com boom (p. 10).
*Venture Capital Mechanics:* Venture capital historically backed semiconductors/minicomputers (late 1950s–1960s), biotech/PCs (late 1970s), and the Internet/Web (1990s) (p. 10). Since the early 1970s, 42% of public U.S. firms received venture capital, accounting for 85% of total public firm R&D spending (p. 11). During the 1996–2000 boom, Benchmark Capital's $5M investment in eBay expanded 1,500-fold in two years (p. 11). One-year classic venture capital returns peaked at 143% in Q3 2000 (p. 11).
*Bubble Correction & Long-Term Value:* The Internet bubble burst in 2000, causing widespread startup failures and job losses (p. 11). Bill Gates noted at Davos (1999): "Look, you bozos, of course they're a bubble, but you're all missing the point. This bubble is attracting so much new capital..." (p. 11). By August 9, 2005 (10th anniversary of Netscape's IPO), the combined market capitalization of four internet leaders (Google, eBay, Yahoo, Amazon) reached ~$200 billion, exceeding total venture capital and IPO funds raised across all internet startups through 2000 (p. 11).

### Creative Destruction
*Disruption of Print Newspapers:* Internet commercialization triggered creative destruction in traditional news media (p. 11).
- *Permanent Closures:* *Rocky Mountain News*, *Tucson Citizen* ceased operations (p. 11).
- *Digital Transition:* *Christian Science Monitor*, *Seattle Post-Intelligencer* eliminated print editions to become web-only publications (p. 11).
- *Magazine & Chain Insolvencies:* *Newsweek* ended its 80-year print run on Dec 31, 2012 (p. 11). Major newspaper chains filed for bankruptcy: Tribune Company, Minneapolis *Star Tribune*, Philadelphia Newspapers, Sun-Times Media Group (p. 11).
- *Industry Trade Closure:* *Editor & Publisher* (125-year-old newspaper trade journal) shut down in 2009 (p. 11).

### Causes of the Entrepreneurial Revolution
*Post-Depression Corporate Mentality:* The Great Depression (October 1929 stock crash) resulted in >13 million unemployed Americans and a 31% GNP decline by 1932 (p. 12). Post-WWII economic growth led workers to prioritize job security, benefits, and long-term employment within large corporations (p. 12). Cultural texts reinforced corporate conformity: William Whyte's *The Organization Man* (1956), John Kenneth Galbraith's *The New Industrial State* (1967), and Jean-Jacques Servan-Schreiber's *The American Challenge* (1968) (p. 12).
*Academic Neglect of Entrepreneurship:* Schumpeter's 1942 book *Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy* predicted capitalism's decline via managerial centralization (p. 12-13). His 1911 work *The Theory of Economic Development* (endorsing entrepreneurship) was largely unstudied in 1970s universities, which emphasized Marxist economics (p. 12-13). Empirical recognition of rising 1970s startup activity occurred retrospectively (Blau 1987; Evans & Leighton 1987) (p. 13).
*GEM Macroeconomic Framework Model (Figure 1.1):*
- *Dual Driving Engines:* National economic growth depends on two interrelated sectors: (1) established firms, and (2) the entrepreneurial process (p. 13).
- *Core Mechanism:* **entrepreneurial opportunities** (perceived market gaps) match with **entrepreneurial capacity** (skills and motivation), producing new venture creation and the phase-out of obsolete firms via **business churning** (p. 13-14).
- *Environmental Context:* **entrepreneurial framework conditions** dictate environmental support (financial availability, government policy/programs, R&D transfer, infrastructure, education/training, social norms, market openness) (p. 14).

### Changes in the Entrepreneurial Framework Conditions
*1. Cultural and Social Norms:*
- *Demographic Shift:* Baby boomers came of age in the 1960s with no living memory of the Great Depression (p. 14).
- *Corporate Workforce Decline:* Fortune 500 companies employed 20% of the U.S. workforce in the 1960s; this share fell continuously starting in 1980, dropping to ~10% by 2005 (p. 14).
- *Executive Exodus:* Corporate downsizing led ~20% of laid-off executives to launch businesses (George Gendron, *Inc.*) (p. 14). Retained executives initiated a "second exodus" away from stagnant corporate hierarchies (p. 14).
- *Meritocracy:* Expansion in business ownership among women, Asian American, Hispanic American, and African American founders ("the last meritocracy") (p. 14).
*2. Government Policy & Market Openness:*
- *Capital Drought:* Early 1970s corporate bailouts (Penn Central Railroad, Lockheed, Chrysler) coincided with a collapse in startup capital; new venture capital commitments fell to $10 million in 1975 (p. 14).
- *1978 Legislative Interventions:* Congress cut capital gains taxes and relaxed ERISA "prudent man" rules, permitting pension fund allocations to venture funds (p. 14-15). Venture capital inflows rose to $4.9 billion by 1987; annual portfolio investments increased 16-fold from $250M (1975) to $3.9B (1987) (p. 15).
- *Deregulation & Antitrust:* Late 1970s airfreight and passenger airline deregulation were followed by the early 1980s antitrust breakup of AT&T (p. 15).
*3. R&D Transfer:*
- *Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR):* Enacted in 1983, SBIR sets aside ~$2.2 billion annually for small-business high-risk technology R&D (p. 15). Through 2015, SBIR funding produced 70,000 issued patents, ~700 public companies, and $41B in follow-on venture capital (funding early developments for Symantec, Qualcomm, DaVinci, iRobot) (p. 15).
- *Federally Funded R&D Innovations:* Taxol cancer treatment (NCI), automated DNA sequencers (Human Genome Project), StormVision weather radar, Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA) tests, V-Chip broadcast controls (p. 15-16).
- *Bayh-Dole Act (1980):* Allowed universities to retain title to patents resulting from federally funded research (p. 16). University patent grants rose from ~300/year pre-1980 to ~10,000 applications in 2003; university tech transfer offices expanded from 25–30 to >1,200 (p. 16). Created ~2,000 new companies, 260,000 jobs, and $40B annual economic output (p. 16).
*4. Physical Infrastructure:* Low-cost IT, cloud infrastructure, mobile communications, logistics networks, and global accelerators/incubators (investing ~$200 million annually worldwide) (p. 16-17).
*5. Human Infrastructure & Education:* Specialized advisory networks, incubators, accelerators, and legal/financial service providers (p. 17). Business plan competitions launched at Babson College and UT Austin in 1985; today >60% of four-year U.S. colleges offer entrepreneurship courses (p. 17).
*6. Financial Framework:*
- *Venture Capital Growth:* NVCA reported U.S. venture capital funding reached $131 billion in 2018 (surpassing the dot-com peak) (p. 17).
- *Crowdfunding:* Kickstarter facilitated >$4 billion across >150,000 projects (p. 17-18).
- *Informal Capital:* Family, friends, and business angels invest >$100 billion annually in startups; 5% of U.S. adults report funding someone else's startup within the prior three years (p. 18).

### Churning and Economic Growth
*Net Firm Dynamics:* Between 1990 and 2000, 11 employer startups were created for every 10 business closures in the U.S. (p. 18).
*Industry Churning Case Studies:*
- *Software Evolution:* VisiCalc -> Lotus 1-2-3 -> Microsoft Excel (p. 18).
- *PC Manufacturing:* In May 1982, DEC introduced the Rainbow PC alongside four rival personal computers; >200 manufacturers entered the PC market in the early 1980s (p. 18). DEC (2nd largest computer maker in 1982) was acquired by Compaq, which merged with HP. IBM sold its PC division in 2004 to Lenovo (founded in 1984 by Chinese Academy of Sciences academics) (p. 18). PCs eliminated typewriters and altered office administrative labor (p. 18).
- *Airlines Post-Deregulation:* Southwest Airlines became the most profitable U.S. carrier; 1971 incumbents failed or consolidated (Continental entered Chapter 11 in 1983/1990 before merging with United) (p. 18). Chapter 11 filings occurred across United, US Airways, Hawaiian, ATA, Delta, Northwest, Aloha, and American; only a fraction of ~100 post-1978 startup airlines survived (p. 18).
- *Disrupted Intermediaries:* Travel agencies, fax machines, video rental stores, CD retailers, film camera manufacturing (p. 18).
*Macroeconomic Consumer Benefits:* Schumpeter noted competition "strikes not at the margins of the profits... but at their foundations and very lives" (p. 18).
- *Walmart Price Impact:* Economists estimate Walmart reduced U.S. consumer inflation by 20%–25% during the 1990s (Prof. Nelson Lichtenstein: Walmart reversed a 100-year history of manufacturer dominance over retailers) (p. 18-19).
- *Airline Deregulation Savings:* Saved passengers $20 billion annually (Alfred Kahn) (p. 19).
- *Communications:* Skype and VoIP provided free global voice services (p. 19).

### Global Entrepreneurship Monitor
*Research Scope:* Established in 1997 by Babson College and London Business School, GEM evaluates global population-level entrepreneurial activity across 99 economies (representing ~95% of world GDP and 85% of world population) (p. 19). The 2018/2019 report analyzed 49 economies (p. 19).
*Primary Research Objectives:* Measure national startup activity, analyze underlying national framework characteristics, and evaluate links between startup activity and national economic growth (p. 19-20).
*Core Activity Classifications:*
- **Total Early-Stage Entrepreneurial Activity (TEA)**: Percentage of adults (aged 18–64) active as nascent entrepreneurs, new business owner-managers, or both (p. 20).
- **nascent entrepreneurs**: Individuals actively attempting to launch a new venture who have not paid salaries for more than three months (p. 19).
- **owner-managers**: Individuals owning and managing a operational business between 3 and 42 months (3.5 years) old (p. 19).
- *Sub-Motivations:* **TEA (opportunity)** measures those starting firms to exploit a perceived opportunity (**improvement-driven opportunity motivated** seek higher income or independence) (p. 19). **TEA (necessity)** tracks **necessity-driven entrepreneurs** starting firms because alternative work options are absent or unsatisfactory (p. 19-20).

> [!NOTE]
> *Lecture vs. Textbook Data Comparison:* Session 1 slides cite SBA data indicating that ~44% of U.S. GDP comes from small businesses and ~46% of the private workforce is employed by small/new businesses (slide 16). In contrast, the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) data in the textbook measures active early-stage participation (**Total Early-Stage Entrepreneurial Activity** or **TEA**), tracking nascent entrepreneurs and new business owners up to 3.5 years old (p. 19-20). The figures do not directly match because SBA data includes all established small businesses (up to 500 employees), whereas GEM's TEA focuses specifically on new venture creation and early-stage dynamics. GEM's TEA metric is more precise for measuring active startup entrepreneurship, while SBA metrics reflect the broader static small business sector.

### Principal Findings from GEM
*Global Scope:* Compiled data across 49 economies spanning low-, middle-, and high-income levels (p. 20).
*Methodological Innovations:*
- **National Entrepreneurship Context Index (NECI)**: Composite metric evaluating environment health across 12 framework conditions (p. 20).
- *Expanded Metrics:* Global tracking of gig/sharing economy participation, family business ownership, **Entrepreneurial Employee Activity (EEA)**, and solo entrepreneurship (p. 20).

### Activity
*Economic Development Correlations:*
- *Low GDP per Capita Economies:* Exhibit high TEA rates, driven by a large proportion of necessity-motivated startups (p. 20).
- *High GDP per Capita Economies:* Exhibit lower overall TEA rates, dominated by opportunity-motivated startups (p. 20).
*Regional TEA Rankings:* Highest average TEA rates occur in Middle East/Africa and Latin America/Caribbean (Angola and Guatemala ranked highest in TEA in their respective regions) (p. 20). East/South Asia shows mixed TEA rates (Thailand high; China and India lower) (p. 20).
*Entrepreneurial Employee Activity (EEA):* High-income economies lead in intrapreneurship (Canada, Ireland, U.S. report EEA >8% of adult population) (p. 20). Europe exhibits the highest relative EEA rates (in Sweden and Germany, entrepreneurship occurs as frequently within existing corporations as in standalone startups) (p. 20).

### Necessity, Opportunity, and Gender
*Development Trajectory:* Necessity-driven motives dominate in low-income economies; as GDP per capita increases, necessity motives decline and improvement-driven opportunity motives dominate (p. 20-21).
*Gender Distribution:* Across 49 economies, only six achieved statistical gender parity in TEA rates: Indonesia, Thailand (East/South Asia); Panama (Latin America); Qatar, Madagascar, Angola (Middle East/Africa) (p. 21).

### Age Distribution of Early-Stage Entrepreneurial Activity
*Peak Career Brackets:* Startup activity peaks globally among early-career (age 25–34) and mid-career (age 35–44) adults who combine ambition with accumulated experience, networks, and resources (p. 22).
*Comparative Age-Driven Profiles (Figure 1.4):*
- *Youth-Driven Startup Economies (18–24 Peak with Steep Subsequent Decline):* Canada, Brazil, Slovak Republic, Greece, Sweden (p. 22-25).
- *Late-Career Startup Economies (Peak Rates Among Older Cohorts):* Republic of Korea, Cyprus, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Sudan (p. 22-25).
- *Underlying Drivers:* Youth-driven profiles reflect low opportunity costs ("little to lose"), peer norms, and early career motivation; older-driven profiles leverage accumulated industry wisdom, networks, capital, or corporate dissatisfaction (p. 25).

### Growth Expectations and Job Creation
*Quality vs. Quantity:* TEA measures startup volume; job growth expectations measure economic quality and employment impact (p. 25).
*Three Job Creation Categories (Figure 1.5):*
1. *Solo Entrepreneurs:* 0 projected jobs over 5 years.
2. *Moderate Growth:* 1 to 5 projected jobs over 5 years.
3. *High Growth:* 6 or more projected jobs over 5 years (p. 26).
*Regional Expectations:*
- *Brazil:* Dominated by solo entrepreneurs with 0 job creation expectations (p. 26).
- *Middle East & Africa:* Few entrepreneurs in Madagascar and Lebanon expect to add >6 jobs; nearly 25% of entrepreneurs in Sudan and Angola project >6 jobs (p. 26).
- *UAE:* Exhibits low overall TEA, but >50% of active entrepreneurs project high job creation (>6 jobs) (p. 26).

### Entrepreneurship Ecosystems and the Importance of Support
*Definition:* An **entrepreneurial ecosystem** is a multidimensional enterprise supporting startup development through interrelated activities (education, financing, training, public policy, research) among individuals, organizations, and institutions (p. 27).
*12 Evaluated Framework Conditions:*
1. Entrepreneurial financing
2. Government policy support
3. Taxes and bureaucracy
4. Government entrepreneurship programs
5. School-level entrepreneurship education and training
6. Post-school entrepreneurship education and training
7. R&D transfer
8. Access to commercial and professional infrastructure
9. Internal market dynamics
10. Internal market burdens
11. Access to physical and services infrastructure
12. Social and cultural norms (p. 27).
*NECI Rankings & System Interdependence (Table 1.3):* NECI evaluates environment health on a 10-point scale (p. 27-28). Severe weakness in even a few conditions undermines national startup activity despite strengths in other areas (p. 27).
- *Top 6 NECI Country Rankings (2018/19 GEM):*
  1. Qatar (6.7) — High Income, Middle East/Africa
  2. Indonesia (6.6) — Low Income, East/South Asia
  3. Netherlands (6.5) — High Income, Europe/North America
  4. Taiwan (6.3) — High Income, East/South Asia
  5. India (6.2) — Low Income, East/South Asia
  6. United States (6.0) — High Income, Europe/North America (p. 28).
- *Comparative Rankings:* Israel ranked 28th (5.1), United Kingdom 30th (4.9), Colombia 32nd (4.8), Egypt 34th (4.7), Mozambique 54th (3.2) (p. 28).
## Chapter 2: The Entrepreneurial Process

*Context:* Chapter 2 provides an overview of the critical factors that give birth to new enterprises and drive their development from initial idea to sustainable growth (p. 41). An **entrepreneur** perceives an opportunity and creates an organization to pursue it, while the **entrepreneurial process** includes all functions, activities, and actions involved in perceiving opportunities and creating organizations (p. 41).

> [!NOTE]
> *Lecture Mapping Note:* Sessions 1 and 2 present a three-stage structural frame: *Opportunity Discovery → Opportunity Evaluation → Opportunity Exploitation (within a Context)*. This maps directly onto Chapter 2's structure:
> - **Opportunity Discovery** maps to *Critical Factors for Starting a New Enterprise* (Carol Moore's process model covering innovation and triggering events) (p. 42-47).
> - **Opportunity Evaluation** maps to *Evaluating Opportunities for New Businesses* (Timmons Framework balancing opportunity, team, and resources) (p. 47-52).
> - **Opportunity Exploitation** maps to *Determining Resource Needs and Acquiring Resources*, *Profit Potential*, and *Ingredients for a Successful New Business* (bootstrapping, financing, cash flow, and execution) (p. 52-59).

### Critical Factors for Starting a New Enterprise
*Carol Moore's Model of the Entrepreneurial Process (Figure 2.1):* Venture creation progresses across four distinct stages driven by personal, sociological, organizational, and environmental factors:
1. *Innovation Stage:* Triggered by personal factors (need for achievement, locus of control, ambiguity tolerance, risk-taking, personal values, education, experience) and environmental factors (opportunities, role models, culture) (p. 43-44).
2. *Triggering Event Stage:* Promoted by personal factors (job dissatisfaction, job loss, age, gender, commitment), sociological factors (networks, teams, family, role models), and environmental factors (economic conditions, competition, incubator availability, government policy) (p. 43-44).
- *Triggering Case Examples:*
- *Mary Ellen Sheets (Two Men and a Truck):* Started a moving business in the 1980s after her college-bound sons left their moving truck behind; hired two movers to meet ongoing customer requests, expanding the venture to 380 franchises and 2,800+ trucks (p. 43).
- *Brent Schuldecker:* Fired during Pfizer's 2008 plant closure in Terre Haute, IN; partnered with laid-off colleagues to purchase Pfizer equipment and launch a contract laboratory services firm (p. 43).
- *Paul Tasner (PulpWorks, Inc.):* Fired at age 66 from a manufacturing executive role; launched PulpWorks to eliminate toxic plastic packaging via biodegradable molded pulp, doubling revenues annually with zero debt (p. 43).
3. *Implementation Stage:* Driven by personal commitment and vision, organizational structures (team, strategy, culture), and environmental resources (investors, bankers, lawyers, suppliers, customers) (p. 43-44).
4. *Growth Stage:* Guided by leadership capabilities, organizational resources, and changing market conditions (p. 43-44).
*Idea Sources & Industry Experience:* An Inc. 500 study revealed 57% of founders obtained their startup idea directly from their industry of current employment, and 23% from a closely related industry—totaling 80% founded within familiar sectors (p. 44).
- *Serial Entrepreneur Example:* David Neeleman founded Morris Air in 1984 (pioneering electronic ticketing), sold to Southwest, co-founded WestJet, founded JetBlue, and later launched Azul Linhas in Brazil (p. 44).
*Personal Attributes:*
- *Need for Achievement & Control:* Entrepreneurs possess high achievement motivation and an internal **locus of control** (belief that personal actions determine outcomes rather than luck or fate) (p. 44-45).
- **tolerance for ambiguity**: Comfort with unorganized, uncertain, and rapidly shifting environments (p. 45).
- *Risk Taking:* Brockhaus (1980) demonstrated that entrepreneurs do not possess higher risk-taking propensities than corporate managers; they take calculated, managed risks (p. 45).
*Environmental & Sociological Drivers:*
- *Role Models & Geographic Clusters:* Stanford sociologist Everett Rogers identified "Silicon Valley fever," where abundant local success stories make venture creation appear achievable (p. 44-45).
- *Family & Educational Background:* Over 50% of Babson College entrepreneurship undergraduates come from business-owning families; Hiscox data shows 33% of U.S. founders have business-owning parents (p. 45). MIT alumni and faculty companies combined would constitute the 10th largest economy in the world (p. 45).
- *Age Demographics:* Starting at age 25 involves low personal assets and dependents, whereas age 45 involves mortgages and family commitments; an MIT study of 2.7 million founders established that the median founder age is 41.9, and the median age for high-growth founders is 45 (p. 45-46).
- *Demographic Expansion:* Entrepreneurship serves as "the last meritocracy" for women and minority founders (p. 46). Sara Blakely launched Spanx with $5,000 in personal savings while selling fax machines door-to-door, becoming the youngest self-made female billionaire (p. 46-47). Reid Hoffman co-founded LinkedIn in 2003, joining technical vision with CEO Jeff Weiner to build a platform acquired by Microsoft for $26 billion in 2016 (p. 47).

### Evaluating Opportunities for New Businesses
*Small Business Survival Realities:* Folklore asserts 90% of startups fail by year 10; Census Bureau data shows actual 10-year firm survival is approximately 25% (p. 47-48).
*Venture Capital Maxim:* VC pioneer Georges Doriot stated: "Always consider investing in a grade-A man with a grade-B idea. Never invest in a grade-B man with a grade-A idea" (p. 48).
*The Timmons Framework (Figure 2.3):* Formulated by Jeffry Timmons, successful venture creation requires balancing three driving forces under environmental uncertainty:
1. *Opportunity:* Market demand, growth trajectory, and margin structure.
2. *Entrepreneur/Team:* Industry experience, leadership skills, and track record.
3. *Resources:* Capital parsimony and resource control.
- *Fits and Gaps:* The business plan serves as the operational tool to align these three forces and bridge gaps (p. 48-49).
*Evaluation Dimensions:*
- *Customer Validation:* Founders must name specific prospective buyers; hypothetical needs do not constitute markets (p. 49-50). To justify leaving a $70,000 salaried job, a venture typically needs to reach $750,000 in annual revenue to cover overhead, benefits, and market-rate returns (p. 50).
- *Timing & Fads:* Brief market fads carry high inventory risks (e.g., video rental stores shifting from VCR to DVD to streaming) (p. 50-51).
- *Founder Experience:* Industry know-how is the single most important predictor of success, followed by management experience with profit-and-loss (P&L) accountability (p. 51-52).
- *Frugality Example:* Tough Mudder obstacle race was conceptualized by Harvard Business School students in 2010; founders bootstrapped growth with minimal capital assets and low overhead (p. 52).

### Determining Resource Needs and Acquiring Resources
*Resource Focus:* Capital must be concentrated on core competitive drivers (e.g., tech firms prioritize engineering talent and patents; retail prioritizes prime location) (p. 52-53).
- *Southwest Airlines Example:* Launched in 1971 between Dallas, Houston, Austin, and San Antonio by purchasing four unsold Boeing 737s at $4 million each during an airline recession, achieving low cost per mile (p. 53).
*Resource Parsimony:* Controlling resources without owning them via leasing, sub-leasing, or rent-for-equity arrangements (p. 53-54).
*Marginal Cost Bargaining:* Smart founders exploit supplier marginal costs—the cost of producing one additional unit (p. 53-54). Google founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin bought a terabyte of hard drives at discount rates, built custom computer casings out of Lego blocks in a Stanford dorm room, and maxed out personal credit cards before raising angel capital (p. 53-54).
*Capital Structure Options:*
- **debt**: Borrowed funds that preserve 100% equity ownership but require mandatory interest payments and principal repayment (p. 54). Requires asset collateral or personal guarantees (plus ~25% personal equity contribution for SBA-backed loans) (p. 54-55).
- **equity**: Capital raised by selling ownership shares; does not require interest or repayment, but dilutes founder ownership (p. 54).
*Financing Progression:*
1. Self-funding through personal savings and **sweat equity** (ownership earned in lieu of cash wages) (p. 55).
2. Informal investors, including a **business angel** (wealthy individual investing personal funds) or the **four Fs** (founders, family, friends, and fools) (p. 55-56). GEM data (Figure 2.4) shows informal capital sources comprise 40-44% close family, 28% friends/neighbors, 10% work colleagues, and 7-9% strangers (p. 56).
3. Bank lines of credit secured by accounts receivable and inventory (p. 55).
4. Formal venture capital firms and Initial Public Offerings (IPOs) (p. 55-56).
- *Apple Funding Progression:* Steve Jobs sold his Volkswagen bus and Steve Wozniak sold his scientific calculator ($1,300 total) to build Apple I; after HP and Atari rejected the design, angel Armas Markkula Jr. invested $91,000 and secured a Bank of America line of credit (p. 55).

### Profit Potential
*Profit versus Cash Flow:* Accounting profit does not equal cash flow; rapidly growing ventures frequently experience negative operating cash flow due to working capital demands and inventory expansion, requiring equity injections that cause ownership dilution (p. 56-57).
*Cash Metrics:*
- **burn rate**: The negative real-time cash flow from monthly operations (p. 57).
- *Free Cash Flow:* Operating cash generated beyond capital requirements (p. 57).
*Niche Specialization:* Regis McKenna noted that fastest-growing ventures inhabit the "others" segment of market share charts; founders must target specialized niche markets (p. 57).
*Organizational Speed:* Fast decision-making and flat management hierarchies enable small firms to outmaneuver corporate giants (e.g., Walmart, IKEA, Starbucks, Uniqlo) (p. 57).

### Ingredients for a Successful New Business
*Bygrave's Nine Fs for Entrepreneurial Success (Figure 2.5):*
1. **Founders**: Led by a first-class entrepreneur and balanced team (p. 58).
2. **Focused**: Concentrating on specific niche markets (p. 58).
3. **Fast**: Executing decisions and market entry swiftly (p. 58).
4. **Flexible**: Adapting rapidly to customer feedback and market changes (p. 58).
5. **Forever-innovating**: Continually introducing new product and service enhancements (p. 58).
6. **Frugal**: Maintaining low overhead and high productivity (p. 58).
7. **Friendly**: Cultivating strong relationships with buyers, suppliers, and staff (p. 58).
8. **Fun**: Maintaining passion and enjoyment in daily operations (p. 58).
9. **Fortune**: Creating substantial wealth for founders and investors (p. 58).
*Gazelles versus Elephants:* Successful ventures operate as agile "gazelles," avoiding corporate inertia to prevent becoming lumbering "elephants" or extinct "dinosaurs" (p. 58-59).

> [!NOTE]
> *Vocabulary Comparison:* The textbook structures its final section around Bygrave's Nine Fs (Founders, Focused, Fast, Flexible, Forever-innovating, Frugal, Friendly, Fun, Fortune) (p. 58), whereas Session 3 slides present six psychological attributes (determination, energy, drive for financial success, need to achieve, independence, support network). Both sources agree on the primacy of founder capability, achievement motivation, and environmental support networks.

## Chapter Summary
Economic growth is driven by the dual engines of established firms and early-stage entrepreneurial activity, where individuals convert perceived market opportunities into new organizations through structured resource acquisition and iterative market testing.
*Mechanism:* Startup creation begins when personal motivators and environmental triggers prompt a founder to evaluate an opportunity against customer demand, industry experience, and timing windows. ==The founder then balances the Timmons Framework forces—Opportunity, Team, and Resources—by bootstrapping initial operations with sweat equity and informal angel capital to achieve positive cash flow while avoiding destructive ownership dilution.==

## Key Concepts
- **entrepreneur**: A person who perceives an opportunity and creates an organization to pursue it (p. 3).
- **entrepreneurial process**: The functions, activities, and actions associated with perceiving opportunities and creating organizations (p. 3).
- **Moore's Law**: The empirical observation by Gordon Moore that transistor density on integrated circuits doubles roughly every two years (p. 5).
- **Genentech**: The pioneering biotech firm founded by Robert Swanson and Herbert Boyer in 1976 using recombinant DNA technology (p. 8).
- **HTML**: Hypertext Markup Language created by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989 to format and link documents on the World Wide Web (p. 9).
- **entrepreneurial opportunities**: Perceived market gaps or situations where new goods and services can be introduced at a profit (p. 13).
- **entrepreneurial capacity**: The skills, motivation, and capability of individuals within a population to launch ventures (p. 13).
- **business churning**: ==The economic process where outmoded or inefficient firms are phased out as new startups enter the market== (p. 14).
- **entrepreneurial framework conditions**: Environmental factors—including finance, policy, R&D transfer, and culture—that dictate national startup vitality (p. 14).
- **Total Early-Stage Entrepreneurial Activity (TEA)**: The GEM metric measuring the percentage of adults aged 18–64 active as nascent entrepreneurs or new business owners (p. 20).
- **nascent entrepreneurs**: Individuals actively setting up a business who have not paid salaries for more than three months (p. 19).
- **owner-managers**: Individuals owning and managing a business operating between 3 and 42 months (p. 19).
- **TEA (opportunity)**: Early-stage startup activity motivated by desire to exploit a business opportunity or increase independence (p. 19).
- **TEA (necessity)**: Early-stage startup activity driven by a lack of alternative employment options (p. 19-20).
- **National Entrepreneurship Context Index (NECI)**: A composite GEM index rating the health of 12 framework conditions across nations (p. 20).
- **Entrepreneurial Employee Activity (EEA)**: The prevalence of employee-led innovation and intrapreneurship within existing organizations (p. 20).
- **entrepreneurial ecosystem**: A regional network of institutions, capital, policies, and culture that supports venture creation (p. 27).
- **locus of control**: An individual's belief regarding whether life outcomes are controlled by personal effort (internal) or external chance (external) (p. 44).
- **tolerance for ambiguity**: The psychological capacity to operate comfortably in unstructured, uncertain environments (p. 45).
- **debt**: Borrowed startup capital requiring mandatory interest payments and principal repayment without surrendering equity (p. 54).
- **equity**: Ownership capital invested in exchange for venture shares, requiring no fixed repayment schedule (p. 54).
- **sweat equity**: Uncompensated labor and effort contributed by founders in exchange for initial company stock (p. 55).
- **business angel**: A wealthy informal investor who provides personal equity capital to early-stage ventures (p. 55).
- **four Fs**: The informal capital sources comprising founders, family, friends, and foolhardy investors (p. 55-56).
- **burn rate**: The negative real-time monthly cash flow generated by a startup's operations (p. 57).

## Worked Example
*Sara Blakely's Spanx Launch (p. 46-47):* At age 29, Sara Blakely was working as a door-to-door fax machine salesperson in Atlanta with $5,000 in personal savings. Frustrated by unflattering pantyhose lines under white slacks, she cut the feet off her pantyhose, creating a crude **looks-like prototype**. She researched hosiery patents at the Georgia Tech library and wrote her own patent application to avoid spending $3,000 in legal fees. Facing repeated rejections from male hosiery mill owners in North Carolina who dismissed the concept, she persisted until one owner's daughters convinced him of the product's market potential. Blakely packaged the footless pantyhose herself, visited Neiman Marcus buyers in Atlanta, and personally demonstrated the product in the ladies' room to secure a retail test order. ==By combining extreme frugality, sweat equity, direct customer validation, and personal sales hustling without taking outside venture capital, Blakely bootstrapped Spanx into a billion-dollar apparel empire.==
