# Lecture - 2 — Session 2: What Does It Take to Be an Entrepreneur (Mon 9/14)
Source: real slide deck `2026 f3015 S2 What does it take to be an entrepreneurs Final.pptx` (16 slides), read in full 2026-09-21. Reading: Ch 1 & 2, per [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Textbook Map|Textbook Map]].
## What you must be able to do
- [[20_Progress/Degree/_Courses/Technical Interview/CSCI 4041/Textbook/Chapter - 1 & 2]]
- Self-assess against the 10-statement entrepreneurial-mindset quiz and place your own score band honestly.
- Name the five paradoxes Zahra uses to describe entrepreneurs and explain why each one is a real tension, not a contradiction to resolve.
- Distinguish the four candidate explanations for "why some people are more entrepreneurial than others" (traits, resources, culture, cognitive biases, prior experience) instead of defaulting to "personality."
- Name at least three sociological/environmental factors that build a supportive entrepreneurial environment.
- Explain what a serial entrepreneur gains from repeated ventures beyond the money.
## Key ideas (short)
- The **entrepreneurial-mindset quiz** isn't a personality test with a pass/fail line — it's a self-check against behaviors (selling something before, tolerating imperfection, noticing "there should be a better way") rather than traits.
- Entrepreneurs live inside **paradoxes**, not resolved contradictions: risk-taker *and* risk-averse, overconfident *and* humble enough to pivot, independent *and* reliant on others. The paradox framing matters because it rules out "just be more risk-tolerant" as useful advice.
- The **discovery → evaluation → exploitation → context** diagram reappears from Session 1, now framing the "why are some people more entrepreneurial" question — differences trace to traits, resources, culture, cognitive bias, and prior experience, not one dominant factor.
- A **supportive environment** is built from networks (family, role models, advisors, colleagues), geography, and employer — entrepreneurship is not a solo-personality outcome.

## Examples worth keeping
- **The 10-statement mindset quiz** (Slides 3-4), self-scored: "I enjoy solving problems that don't have a clear answer," "I've come up with a random business idea while showering, walking, or procrastinating," "I'm more excited by risk than afraid of it," "I've sold something before — even if it was lemonade, crafts, tutoring, or digital art," "I often notice problems around me and think, 'There should be a better way to do this.'" Scoring bands: 8-10 true = "born hustler with a founder mindset," 5-7 true = "strong entrepreneurial potential," 0-4 true = "entrepreneurship is a skill anyone can grow."
- **"Fix My Day" exercise** (groups of 3): identify a real student-life frustration from the last 24-48 hours, design a rough solution in a 10-minute group brainstorm, then deliver a 1-minute rapid pitch (problem / solution / who'd use it) to the class.
- **The five paradoxes** (Slide 11, verbatim structure): risk-taker vs. risk-averse; overconfident vs. humble enough to change course; innovative vs. conservative with resources; independent vs. reliant on others; dreamer/optimist vs. realistic in execution.
## Lecture
### 1. Agenda
Three stated topics: Entrepreneurial Attributes, Entrepreneurial Environment, Entrepreneurial Process — tagged directly to Chapters 1 & 2.
### 2. Entrepreneurial-mindset self-quiz
Ten true/untrue statements split across two slides (see Examples above for the sharpest five), followed by a scoring key: 8-10 true = "born hustler," 5-7 true = "strong entrepreneurial potential, keep exploring," 0-4 true = "entrepreneurship is a skill anyone can grow" — a deliberately non-gatekeeping framing (nobody is told they can't be entrepreneurial).
### 3. "Fix My Day" — real-world problem solving
Three-part live exercise in groups of 3: (a) individually jot down "one small but annoying problem you faced in the last 24-48 hours"; (b) 10-minute group brainstorm choosing one problem and generating 2-3 rough solutions, explicitly encouraged to be wild rather than realistic; (c) 5-minute rapid pitch per group answering what's the problem, what's the solution, who would use it. This is the same discovery-stage move the course will repeat in Session 4's ideation exercises and Session 5's opportunity evaluation.
### 4. The process diagram, reused
Opportunity Discovery → Opportunity Evaluation → Opportunity Exploitation, inside Context — shown again verbatim from Session 1, now used as the frame for the next section's question.
### 5. The five paradoxes of entrepreneurs
See Examples above for the full list. Framing line: entrepreneurs hold both sides of each tension at once — this is presented as the *normal* condition, not a flaw to train out.
### 6. Why are some people more entrepreneurial than others? Nature vs. nurture
Direct question posed to the class, then answered with five candidate factors (Slide 13): personality traits (risk tolerance, openness to experience), access to resources (financial capital, networks), cultural and social influences, cognitive biases (optimism, overconfidence), and prior experience (exposure to entrepreneurship, industry knowledge). No single factor is privileged — the slide lists all five as co-explanations.
### 7. What makes a supportive entrepreneurial environment?
Answered with: networks (parents/family, role models, advisors, colleagues), geography, and employer — labeled "Sociological and Environmental Factors" on the slide.
### 8. Serial entrepreneurs
Two open questions posed with no slide answer given: "Why do they do it?" and "What do they learn from experience?" — left as class-discussion prompts, not resolved in the deck itself.

## Lecture-to-textbook synthesis
==Entrepreneurs operate inside five real paradoxes at once — risk-taking and risk-averse, overconfident and humble, independent and reliant on others — and this tension, not a fixed personality profile, is what nature-vs-nurture research and the course's own environment factors are trying to explain.==
*Mechanism:* If entrepreneurship were a single stable trait, the nature/nurture question would have a clean answer. Instead the slide gives five co-existing factors (traits, resources, culture, cognitive bias, prior experience) precisely because the paradoxes mean no one trait dominates — someone can be risk-tolerant by personality but risk-averse by environment (limited resources), and the outcome depends on which factor is load-bearing in their specific case.
- Lecture example/scenario: the "Fix My Day" exercise puts the discovery stage into practice under exactly this tension — groups are told to "encourage wild ideas, the goal is to think creatively, not realistically (yet)," then immediately forced into a realistic 1-minute pitch, enacting the dreamer-vs-realist paradox live.
- Textbook connection: [[20_Progress/Degree/_Courses/Technical Interview/CSCI 4041/Textbook/Chapter - 1 & 2]] — Chapter 2's "Ingredients for a Successful New Business" is the book's structured answer to the same "why some succeed" question the nature/nurture slide poses informally.

# Lecture - 3 — Session 3: The Entrepreneurial Personality (Wed 9/16)
Source: real slide deck `2026 S3015 S3 Entrepreneurial Persoanlity.pptx` (25 slides), read in full 2026-09-21. Reading: Ch 2 continued, per [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Textbook Map|Textbook Map]].
## What you must be able to do
- [[20_Progress/Degree/_Courses/Technical Interview/CSCI 4041/Textbook/Chapter - 1 & 2]]
- Take the 7-question personality quiz and correctly classify your own dominant type (Explorer, Architect, Connector, Steward) plus its named risk.
- List the six named entrepreneurial attributes (determination, motivation drivers, energy, desire for financial success, need to achieve, independence, support network) and give one line on why each matters.
- Explain why "necessary but insufficient" is the right way to describe entrepreneurial personality, not "sufficient on its own."
- State the four sub-components of "what motivates entrepreneurs" (need for achievement, need for recognition, self-efficacy, locus of control) without collapsing them into one idea.
## Key ideas (short)
- Entrepreneurial personality is **necessary but insufficient** — a real, explicit callout on the slide, not a throwaway line. Having the traits doesn't guarantee success; lacking them makes success much harder.
- The **quiz's four types** (Explorer, Architect, Connector, Steward) aren't ranked — each has a named strength and a named risk, so the quiz isn't sorting "good vs. bad" entrepreneurs, it's sorting *styles*.
- **Determination** gets singled out as the single most-emphasized attribute on the slide, backed by two separate Steve Jobs quotes — perseverance, not raw talent, is framed as the differentiator.
- Motivation itself splits into four distinct drivers (achievement, recognition, self-efficacy, locus of control) — conflating them loses the point that these can pull in different directions for the same person.

## Examples worth keeping
- **The 7-question personality quiz** (Slides 5-11), each with four lettered responses mapped consistently to a type across all seven questions: **A = Explorer** (act first, adjust as you learn), **B = Architect** (analyze, reduce uncertainty through data), **C = Connector** (share risk, mobilize networks), **D = Steward** (wait for favorable odds, respect boundaries).
- **Scoring key** (Slide 12-13): Mostly A = "The Explorer — thrives on action/experimentation, risk: moving too fast without reflection." Mostly B = "The Architect — approaches entrepreneurship as design/strategy, risk: analysis paralysis." Mostly C = "The Connector — builds momentum through networks/trust, risk: over-reliance on others." Mostly D = "The Steward — emphasizes stability/discipline/sustainability, well-suited for scaling/governance/family business, risk: missing transformative opportunities."
- Two Steve Jobs quotes used back to back: on determination — "I'm convinced that about half of what separates the successful entrepreneurs from the non-successful ones is pure perseverance"; on achievement — "Being the richest man in the cemetery doesn't matter to me... going to bed at night saying we've done something wonderful... that's what matters to me."
## Lecture
### 1. Agenda
Personality quiz, entrepreneurial attributes, discussion of the Profile assignment — tagged to Chapter 2.
### 2. Entrepreneurial personality quiz (7 questions)
Full question set, each testing a different decision point: (Q2) first reaction to a frustrating problem, (Q3) comfort with uncertainty, (Q4) how you treat failure, (Q5) behavior under scarce resources, (Q6) what you focus on when pitching, (Q7) attitude toward rules/institutions, (Q8) what energizes you most. See Examples above for the full A/B/C/D → type mapping and scoring key.
### 3. Entrepreneurial personality, framed
Single-word slide title, followed immediately by the speaker-note framing: "necessary but insufficient" — the personality types above matter, but the deck pivots straight into naming personality's limits before listing the traits that supposedly define it.
### 4. Has determination
Presented as one of the most important characteristics, with the Jobs perseverance quote (see Examples) and a direct challenge: "Do you have 'it' in you?" — this rhetorical question repeats across three later slides (Energy, Financial Success), functioning as the deck's recurring self-check device.
### 5. What motivates entrepreneurs? (four factors)
Posed as an open question, then answered on the next slide with four named attributes: **need for achievement**, **need for recognition**, **self-efficacy**, **locus of control**. No elaboration given per-factor in the deck — these read as headline terms the textbook chapter is expected to define in depth.
### 6. Is energetic
Requires long work hours; frequently unable to control the number of hours; must respond to the job's demands. Same "do you have 'it' in you?" close.
### 7. Wants financial success
Duplicated across two consecutive slides (14 and 15 of this section) — same content both times: financial success as a primary reason most entrepreneurs cite for going into business, same "do you have 'it' in you?" close. Likely a slide-deck duplication artifact rather than intentional repetition; flagged, not silently deduplicated.
### 8. Has a need to achieve
Entrepreneurs strive to excel and set quite high objectives — willingness to set high goals and enjoy striving toward them. Backed by the second Jobs quote (see Examples), which reframes achievement away from wealth and toward the act of building something.
### 9. Seeks independence
Likes being independent and in control of situations, drawn to trying new things — the shortest of the attribute slides, no quote or elaboration.
### 10. Has a support network
A successful entrepreneur needs a support network — the more positive support from family/friends, the more the entrepreneur can concentrate on making the business succeed. Framed as an external attribute (who surrounds you) rather than an internal trait, distinct from the other five.
### 11. Integration — closing discussion questions
Two open prompts with no slide answer: "What do these entrepreneurs have in common?" and "Are there negative attributes for entrepreneurial personality? If so, what?" — the second question is a real pivot, since every attribute up to this point was framed positively.

## Lecture-to-textbook synthesis
==Entrepreneurial personality is necessary but insufficient: the course names six real attributes (determination, motivation, energy, drive for financial success, need to achieve, independence, support network), but frames every one of them as a starting condition, not a guarantee.==
*Mechanism:* The "necessary but insufficient" framing does real work — it explains why the course spends far more time later on process (opportunity evaluation, business models, financing) than on personality. Personality gets one session; execution gets the rest of the semester. The four-type quiz reinforces this by refusing to rank styles — Explorer, Architect, Connector, and Steward are all viable, each with a named failure mode, which only makes sense if personality is a starting style, not a success predictor.
- Lecture example/scenario: the two Steve Jobs quotes are doing different jobs — the determination quote argues perseverance beats talent, the achievement quote argues purpose beats wealth. Together they push back against "wants financial success" being the deck's own primary framing two slides earlier — a real internal tension in the source, not resolved by the deck itself.
- Textbook connection: [[20_Progress/Degree/_Courses/Technical Interview/CSCI 4041/Textbook/Chapter - 1 & 2]] — "Ingredients for a Successful New Business" should be where the book gives the fuller, referenced version of these six attributes.

> Don't treat the four-type quiz (Explorer/Architect/Connector/Steward) as a validated psychometric instrument — it's a seven-question classroom exercise with no cited source or reliability data, unlike the Granovetter weak-ties research that Session 4 cites by name. Use it as a self-reflection prompt, not a fact.

> [!SUMMARY]
> Session 3 lists real, specific entrepreneurial attributes but explicitly undercuts its own list with "necessary but insufficient" — the personality profile only sets a starting point; the rest of the course is about what you do with it.
