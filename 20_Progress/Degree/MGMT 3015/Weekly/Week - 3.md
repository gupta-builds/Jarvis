# Lecture - 4 — Session 4: Creativity and Idea Generation (Mon 9/21)
Source: real slide deck `2026  f3015 S4 Idea Generation.pptx` (32 slides), read in full 2026-09-21 — today's session. Reading: Ch 3 & 4, per [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Textbook Map|Textbook Map]]. **The Profile of a Successful Entrepreneur (5%) is due today** — see [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Assignments/Profile of a Successful Entrepreneur|Profile of a Successful Entrepreneur]].
## What you must be able to do
- [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Chapter - 3 & 4|Chapter - 3 & 4]]
- Name the three ideation methods (watch/listen for opportunity, use your networks, flash of insight) and give one concrete tactic for each.
- Explain the strength-of-weak-ties finding (Granovetter 1973) and why affiliation networks underperform efficiency networks for innovation.
- List the five named barriers to creativity and recognize the self-talk phrases the slide uses to illustrate each.
- Distinguish an idea from a business concept (the session's own framing, distinct from Session 1's idea-vs-opportunity distinction).
## Key ideas (short)
- Creativity is **learnable and structural**, not innate — the deck opens by contrasting what school does to *promote* vs. *stifle* creativity, framing the session as a corrective, not a talent showcase.
- The **three ideation methods** (watch/listen, networks, flash of insight) are presented as parallel, exhaustive-enough starting points — each gets its own exercise, not just a definition.
- **Weak ties beat strong ties for innovation**, and *no prior relationship* beats weak ties — a real, cited, counterintuitive research finding (Granovetter 1973), not a generic "networking matters" claim.
- **Barriers to creativity are mostly self-talk**, not external constraints — "I am not ready," "it is not the right time," "where I come from, people do not do this" — naming them is the deck's actual intervention.

## Examples worth keeping
- **The paperclip exercise**: in 5 minutes, list 10+ uses for a paperclip, including unconventional ones — a classic divergent-thinking warm-up.
- **The chair-and-skateboard combination exercise**: combine two unrelated objects into a new invention and describe its uses — direct instance of the "creative combination" ideation method.
- **The stress-reduction app exercise**: design an app that reduces stress, describing features, mechanism, and why it would work.
- **Steve Jobs on creativity** (Wired, 1996): "Creativity is just connecting things. When you ask creative people how they did something, they feel a little guilty because they didn't really do it, they just saw something... they were able to connect experiences they've had and synthesize new things." Zahra's own speaker note: "One of the reasons I'm asking you to keep a journal."
- **The Apple Macintosh as a worked flash-of-insight example**: Xerox's graphical user interface + Apple's small machine = the Macintosh — shown as a literal equation on the slide.
- **Google's founding as a second flash-of-insight example**: data mining + algorithms + Altavista's full-text web search + Overture's ad-search model = the search-plus-ads business (unnamed on the slide, but the components map directly to Google's early history).
- **The Strength of Weak Ties** (Granovetter 1973): affiliation networks (dense, small, non-diverse, strong ties — family, friends) underperform efficiency networks (sparse, large, diverse, weak ties — acquaintances) for innovation. The slide adds a second-order claim: teams with *no prior relationships* outperform both.
## Lecture
### 1. Agenda
Where creative ideas come from (3 ideation methods, 3 exercises), team ideation time, Case 4 discussion, tagged to Chapters 3 & 4.
### 2. Creativity — what school does to it
Two-sided framing slide: "2 things school does to promote your creativity" paired with "2 things school does to stifle your creativity" (the specific items weren't itemized in the extracted slide text — check the original deck or in-class discussion for the actual four points).
### 3. Who do you know who's creative?
Individual reflection: name someone creative you know, write 3-5 things they do that demonstrate it — a warm-up before the group exercises.
### 4. Three divergent-thinking exercises
The paperclip, chair-and-skateboard, and stress-reduction-app exercises (see Examples above) — three separate prompts, each testing a different creative move (listing/fluency, combination, applied design).
### 5. Team ideation exercise: creative combination
Groups randomly pick a number 1-20, identify the corresponding object from a reference image, and must combine that item into one new, useful product — one team member presents.
### 6. Barriers to creativity
Five named barriers: social approval, fear of failure, past experiences, and two self-talk phrases treated as barriers in their own right — "I am not ready" and "it is not the right time... where I come from, people do not do this." Followed by a group exercise: share which barrier blocks your own creativity, then brainstorm one or two ways to overcome it.
### 7. How to improve your creativity
Seven stated tactics: listen to your inner voice, let your imagination soar, dream the "impossible" dream, imagine "what if," surround yourself with "better" people, surround yourself with people who have different skills, do different things.
### 8. Idea vs. business concept
Named distinction, no elaboration captured in the extracted slide text — likely developed live in discussion. Distinct from Session 1's idea-vs-opportunity framing: this session's cut is about maturity/specificity of the concept, not value/sustainability.
### 9. Sources of ideas for opportunities
Five named sources: consumers, existing products and services, distribution channels, federal government, research and development.
### 10. Opportunities, redefined
Restated equation: **Entrepreneurship = Opportunity + recognition + evaluation + exploitation**. Two follow-up questions posed directly: "what is an opportunity?" (idea and opportunity are related but different) and "what makes an opportunity attractive?" — left open, feeding into Session 5.
### 11. Ideation Method 1 — Watch/Listen for Opportunity
Framed as the class's **first Journal Entry, due ASAP and ongoing**. Four prompting contexts given: visiting a store/restaurant (what would make the visit more pleasant/productive?), extracurricular activities (how could a new product improve the experience?), at work (how might products/services/processes improve for customers and employees?), anywhere (record observations of people around you). A structured journal template is given: inspiration source, problem/challenge solved, who it affects, its cost (time/dollars), a proposed solution, and a simple sketch or service-steps list.
### 12. Ideation Method 2 — Use Your Networks
Opens with a working definition of "network": everyone you know, everyone you've known, everyone who knows you — qualified by two conditions: you like them, and you'd ask them for a favor. Then the Granovetter weak-ties research (see Examples above) with the affiliation-vs-efficiency-network contrast and the no-prior-relationship finding.
### 13. Network exercises
Exercise 1: introduce yourself to 3 new classmates and learn something new about each (hometown, hobbies, goals, a big achievement) — speaker note suggests groups of 4. Exercise 2: interview an actual entrepreneur, with six specific questions given — what triggered their pursuit of entrepreneurship (personal or sociological), their initial goals, whether they're still pursuing/attaining them, how intent changed over time and why, what advice they'd offer, and what other contacts/resources they'd recommend.
### 14. Ideation Method 3 — Flash of Insight
Opens with the Jobs "connecting things" quote (see Examples), then the left-brain/right-brain framing (analytical vs. creative — presented with a question mark, i.e. flagged as a simplification, not endorsed outright). Two worked examples follow: the Macintosh (Xerox GUI + Apple's small machine) and an unnamed second example built from data mining + algorithms + Altavista's full-text search + Overture's ad search — components that map to Google's actual founding, though the slide doesn't name the company.
### 15. Takeaways slide
Three ideation methods restated (watch/listen, networks via weak ties, creative combination/flash of insight) plus a fourth pointer: seek out entrepreneurship resources on campus, named contact — John Stavig, Program Director, Gary S. Holmes Center for Entrepreneurship, CSOM 2-212C.

## Lecture-to-textbook synthesis
==Idea generation runs through three concrete, teachable methods — watch/listen for unmet needs, mine weak-tie networks rather than close ones, and recognize a flash of insight as connecting existing pieces, not inventing from nothing.==
*Mechanism:* Each method targets a different failure mode in how people normally look for ideas. Watching/listening counters the default of only noticing problems that directly inconvenience you. The weak-ties finding counters the instinct to brainstorm only with close friends, who tend to know the same things you do. The flash-of-insight method (Jobs's "connecting things") counters the myth that ideas need to be invented from scratch — the Macintosh example is literally two existing things combined, not a new invention.
- Lecture example/scenario: the creative-combination team exercise (randomly assigned object + forced combination) operationalizes the flash-of-insight method under time pressure, the same way "Fix My Day" (Session 2) operationalized discovery.
- Textbook connection: [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Chapter - 3 & 4|Chapter - 3 & 4]] — Chapter 3's "From Glimmer to Action" section should formalize today's three methods into the book's own named framework.

> [!WARNING]
> Don't treat "flash of insight" as evidence against the "entrepreneurship is learnable" claim from Session 1 — Jobs's own quote explicitly reframes insight as *connecting prior experience*, which is exactly why the deck pairs it with a journal (Method 1) and a network-building habit (Method 2). The three methods are meant to compound, not compete.

# Lecture - 5 — Session 5: Opportunities — Definition & Evaluation (Wed 9/23)
Source: real slide deck `2026 S3015 S5 Opportunities.pptx` (25 slides), read in full 2026-09-28. Speaker notes exist only as bare slide-number placeholders (slides 1-3), no real notes content. Reading: Ch 3 & 4 continued, per [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Textbook Map|Textbook Map]].
## What you must be able to do
- [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Chapter - 3 & 4|Chapter - 3 & 4]]
- State the session's own definition of an entrepreneurial opportunity and name the three forces (technology, market, regulation changes) that create one.
- List the five types of entrepreneurial opportunities (new products, new production processes, new distribution channels, new ways of organizing, new markets) with an example of each.
- Distinguish the four factors that *shape* opportunities (geography, national culture, technology, social change) from the six factors that *trigger recognition* of them (a problem to solve, customer feedback, disappointing experience, technology, experience, serendipity).
- Name the five explanations the deck gives for why some people are more prolific at recognizing opportunities (alertness, prior knowledge, social networks, creativity, entrepreneurial passion).

- State the UN Sustainable Development Goals' three pillars (People, Planet, Prosperity & Partnerships) and explain why SDGs matter for entrepreneurship.
## Key ideas (short)
- An **opportunity** isn't an idea that already feels valuable — it's a situation created by shifts in **technology, markets, or regulation**, and it only becomes real through human judgment and action (the session's own definition, slide 3).
- The deck keeps two questions deliberately separate: what **shapes** an opportunity's existence (geography, culture, technology, social change) versus what **triggers** one person noticing it (a problem, feedback, disappointment, technology, experience, serendipity).
- Some people spot opportunities more often than others — the deck's explanation is **alertness, prior knowledge, social networks, creativity, and entrepreneurial passion**, not luck.
- Evaluating an opportunity means working through concrete, sequential questions — what you're selling, who buys it, how much they spend, what already exists, how you differ — not one gut-check.
- The session pairs a for-profit lens (Mall of America massage service) with a mission lens (**Sustainable Development Goals**) — the same evaluation logic applies to both.

## Lecture
### 1. Session outline
Three-part outline stated directly: how to define opportunities, how to evaluate them, tagged to Chapter 3 & 4.
### 2. Defining an opportunity
Full definition, given in one sentence: "An entrepreneurial opportunity is a situation in which new goods, services, or processes can be introduced and sold at a profit — created by changes in technology, markets, or regulation, and recognized or constructed through human judgment and action."
### 3. Team exercise: the future space industry
Group task: working as a group, identify an entrepreneurial opportunity in the future space industry, explain what the opportunity is and why it's interesting. Market framing given directly (see Examples above). Five niches listed to prime the discussion: satellite data analytics, space debris removal and recycling, low-Earth orbit (LEO) internet infrastructure, in-space manufacturing and 3D printing, lunar and asteroid resource extraction.
### 4. Five types of entrepreneurial opportunities
Named list, reproduced exactly as given: new products, new production processes, new distribution channels, new ways of organizing, new markets.
### 5. How opportunities differ
"Opportunity has multiple dimensions": product/service, customer, market (could be virtual), value being exchanged/created.
### 6. Factors that shape opportunities
Four named factors, each with the deck's own example: bounded by geography (ski lodges), national culture (mid-autumn festival, Thanksgiving, religious holidays), technology (traveling around the world, living in space), social change (fertility clinics).
### 7. Factors that trigger recognition of opportunities
Six named triggers: a problem to be solved (household gadgets), customer feedback (given with its own question mark on the slide — flagged, not stated as settled), disappointing experience (creation of a holiday), technology (FedEx, Apple, "many tech giants"), experience (many medical devices), serendipity.
### 8. Why some people are more prolific at recognizing opportunities
Posed directly as a question: "Some people are prolific in seeing and recognizing opportunities for new ventures. How do you explain this?" Five explanations given: alertness, prior knowledge, social networks, creativity, entrepreneurial passion.
### 9. Not every opportunity is worth pursuing
Transition slide, one question posed and left open into the evaluation exercise: "What criteria should you use to evaluate a business opportunity?"
### 10. Opportunity Evaluation exercise: Mall of America massage service
Scenario: you and your group are considering opening a massage service in the Mall of America — how will you determine if this is a potentially profitable opportunity? The worksheet runs slide by slide:
- What do you plan to make, offer, or sell? What does this include? What does this exclude?
- Will your business be online or in person (traditional)? Why?
- List as many potential customer groups as you can think of (individuals or companies who might be interested), with an "Approach" column for what you'll learn from each approach — then identify the top 3 groups, describing their characteristics and why they'd be interested.
- Focusing on the top 3 groups: how much do they spend on this product type annually, how frequently do they buy it, and how will you appeal to each group?
- List 5 ways to determine demand for the product, again against an "Approach → what will you learn" structure.
- List up to 5 products that already exist and are similar to what you plan to do; rate how similar/different each is; identify any market "gaps."
- List up to 5 ways to differentiate the product from what already exists, why each matters, and how you'll make it happen.
### 11. The Sustainable Development Goals
17 global goals adopted by the United Nations in 2015; aim to end poverty, protect the planet, and ensure prosperity; target year 2030; apply to governments, businesses, and individuals. Three pillars given by name: **People** (poverty, health, education, gender equality), **Planet** (climate action, clean energy, biodiversity), **Prosperity & Partnerships** (innovation, decent work, global cooperation). Why they matter for business and entrepreneurship, four points: create new market and innovation opportunities, guide CSR and ESG strategies, encourage responsible and sustainable growth, enable entrepreneurs to solve social and environmental problems.
### 12. SDG business-idea exercise
Objective stated directly: select one and only one SDG, choose one area within that SDG, and develop an idea for a new business — answering what you will do, why it's needed now and who needs it, how you will make it happen, what key barriers you anticipate, and what key results you anticipate.

## Lecture-to-textbook synthesis
==An idea only becomes an opportunity once it survives a structured evaluation — customer segmentation, demand estimation, competitive gaps, and differentiation — not because it sounds appealing.==
*Mechanism:* The session's own definition ties an opportunity to profit and to forces outside the founder's control (technology, market, regulatory change), recognized through judgment — but that judgment isn't passive. The evaluation worksheet (Lecture sections 10 and 12) forces it into discrete, answerable questions before real commitment, the same escalation-of-commitment logic Chapter 3 states directly (p. 71, 92): low-cost tests should dictate concept changes before capital gets spent.
- Lecture example/scenario: the Mall of America massage-service worksheet runs the full sequence — product definition, delivery channel, top-3 customer groups, demand approaches, up to 5 comparable products and gaps, up to 5 differentiators — and the SDG exercise compresses the same sequence into four questions for a mission-driven idea instead of a for-profit one.
- Textbook connection: [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Chapter - 3 & 4|Chapter - 3 & 4]] — the Five Dimensions of Opportunity Space and the Opportunity Checklist supply the named framework the worksheet operationalizes without naming it.
- Concept links: none yet (concepts out of scope this pass).
> [!WARNING]
> Don't read "factors that shape opportunities" (section 6) and "factors that trigger recognition" (section 7) as the same list restated. Shape explains why an opportunity exists at all — external conditions (geography, culture, technology, social change). Trigger explains why one specific person notices it — a personal moment (a problem, feedback, disappointment, technology, experience, serendipity). Conflating them collapses two questions the deck keeps deliberately separate.
> [!SUMMARY]
> Session 5 turns "is this a good idea?" into a worksheet — the same evaluation logic applied first to a profit-driven service business, then, via the SDGs, to a mission-driven one.
