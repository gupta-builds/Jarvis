# Week - 3
## What you must be able to do
- Using [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Chapter - 3 & 4|Chapter - 3 & 4]], name the three ideation methods from Session 4 (watch and listen for opportunity, use your networks, flash of insight), give one tactic for each, and compare them with the book's four-step idea multiplication (p. 73-76).
- Explain the strength-of-weak-ties finding (Granovetter 1973) and why affiliation networks underperform efficiency networks for innovation.
- List the five named barriers to creativity and recognize the self-talk phrases the slide uses to illustrate them.
- Distinguish an idea from a business concept (Session 4) and an idea from an opportunity (Session 1), and say which question each cut answers.
- State Session 5's definition of an entrepreneurial opportunity, the three forces that create one (technology, markets, regulation), and the five types of opportunity.
- Separate the four factors that shape opportunities (geography, national culture, technology, social change) from the six that trigger recognition of them.
- Apply the opportunity-evaluation worksheet (product definition, delivery channel, customer segments, demand, competitive gaps, differentiation) to a concrete idea and map each step to Chapter 3's Opportunity Checklist.
- Explain what prototyping adds after evaluation (Chapter 4's three-stage process and the minimum viable product), which no lecture in this week covers.
## Key ideas (short)
- Creativity is **learnable and structural**, not innate, and the **three ideation methods** each counter a specific failure: noticing only your own inconvenience, brainstorming only with close friends, and waiting to invent from nothing.
- **Weak ties beat strong ties for innovation**, and teams with *no prior relationship* beat both, a cited finding (Granovetter 1973) and not a generic "networking matters" claim.
- **Barriers to creativity are mostly self-talk** ("I am not ready," "it is not the right time"), so naming them is the deck's actual intervention.
- An **opportunity** is a situation created by shifts in technology, markets, or regulation and made real through human judgment; what **shapes** it (external conditions) and what **triggers** one person to notice it (a personal moment) are deliberately separate questions.
- Evaluating an opportunity is a **sequence of concrete questions** (what you sell, who buys, how much they spend, what already exists, how you differ), not one gut check, and Chapter 3's Opportunity Checklist is the book's named version of that sequence.
- Chapter 4 is **book-only this week**: prototyping and the **minimum viable product** turn assumptions into evidence at the lowest cost, after evaluation and before capital.

### 25. Opportunity Evaluation exercise: Mall of America massage service (Session 5, Wed 9/23)
Scenario: you and your group are considering opening a massage service in the Mall of America — how will you determine if this is a potentially profitable opportunity? The worksheet runs slide by slide:
- What do you plan to make, offer, or sell? What does this include? What does this exclude?
- Will your business be online or in person (traditional)? Why?
- List as many potential customer groups as you can think of (individuals or companies who might be interested), with an "Approach" column for what you'll learn from each approach — then identify the top 3 groups, describing their characteristics and why they'd be interested.
- Focusing on the top 3 groups: how much do they spend on this product type annually, how frequently do they buy it, and how will you appeal to each group?
- List 5 ways to determine demand for the product, again against an "Approach → what will you learn" structure.
- List up to 5 products that already exist and are similar to what you plan to do; rate how similar/different each is; identify any market "gaps."

### Ideation: three lecture methods against the book's four steps
Chapter 3's "From Glimmer to Action" structures ideation as **idea multiplication**, adapted from IDEO and Doug Hall: gather stimuli through **customer anthropology** (observing buyers in their own setting without leading questions), multiply stimuli with "Yes, and..." brainstorming and a sticky-note vote, create customer concepts, and optimize practicality (p. 73-76). Only Session 4's "watch and listen" method overlaps with the first step. The book does not isolate weak ties or flash of insight as named frameworks, and Granovetter's study is cited in the deck but not in the landed chapter note. The book's cases are AllTrails (Russell Cook, p. 72) and Paint Nite (p. 70, 73).
### Evaluation: the named framework behind the worksheet
Session 5's Mall of America worksheet maps onto Chapter 3's **Five Dimensions of Opportunity Space** (customers, competitors, suppliers and vendors, government, global; p. 76-77) and the **Opportunity Checklist** (Figure 3.10, seven categories, p. 91-93). "List customer groups, then narrow to the top 3" mirrors the book's **Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Target Audience** segmentation (p. 77-78), and "list similar products and gaps" mirrors competitor analysis and the **Competitive Profile Matrix** built on key success factors (p. 89-90). Neither source uses the other's vocabulary: the deck never says PTA, STA, or TTA, and the book never frames the material as a fill-in worksheet. Book-only content the deck never touches: the government and supplier dimensions, the **S-curve** of market adoption (p. 82-83), the $750,000 revenue benchmark against a $70,000 forgone salary (p. 80), and the AllTrails, Paint Nite, and Gourmet Stew cases. Chapter 3 also states the **escalation of commitment** logic directly: low-cost early tests should dictate concept changes before substantial capital is committed (p. 71, 92).
### Chapter 4: prototyping, book-only here
Chapter 4 (p. 102-114) defines **prototyping** as quickly building working models to test assumptions, in three stages (represent assumptions, test them, learn and iterate; p. 103-105). It separates low-fidelity from high-fidelity models, looks-like from works-like models, and covers paper prototyping, 3-D printing, Arduino and Raspberry Pi, crowdfunding, **co-creation**, and the **Minimum Viable Product** (Eric Ries; p. 112-113), with Zappos (photos of shoes from local stores, p. 113) and NatureBox (a $22-per-month landing page backed by a Facebook ad drew over 100 sign-ups, p. 118) as cases. Sessions 4 and 5 never reach prototyping. The first lecture contact is the Session 8 and 9 deck's willingness-to-pay tests (MVP, pre-orders, crowdfunding, landing pages with pricing), covered in [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Weekly/Week - 5|Week - 5]].

## Lecture-to-textbook synthesis
==An idea becomes a pursuable opportunity by passing through generation methods and then a structured evaluation of customers, demand, competitors, and differentiation, and the book adds a third step, prototyping, that tests the survivors cheaply before capital is committed.==
*Mechanism:* Each generation method in Session 4 counters a different failure in how people look for ideas: watching and listening corrects noticing only what inconveniences you, weak ties correct brainstorming only with friends who know what you know, and flash of insight corrects the belief that ideas must be invented from scratch (the Macintosh is two existing things combined). Session 5 then forces judgment into discrete, answerable questions before commitment. Chapter 3 states the same escalation-of-commitment logic (p. 71, 92), and Chapter 4 supplies the instrument for it: an MVP maximizes validated learning per dollar (p. 112-113).
- Lecture example/scenario: the Mall of America worksheet asks "list 5 ways to determine demand." NatureBox is the book's executed answer to that question: a landing page and a Facebook ad at $22 per month, over 100 sign-ups, then hand-packed boxes (p. 118). Session 4's random-object combination exercise is the same flash-of-insight method under time pressure.
- Textbook connection: [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Chapter - 3 & 4|Chapter - 3 & 4]]. The Five Dimensions of Opportunity Space and the Opportunity Checklist name what the worksheet does, and Chapter 4 names what comes after it.
- Concept links: [[Concept - Strength of Weak Ties]] (to create), [[Concept - Opportunity Checklist]] (to create), [[Concept - Minimum Viable Product]] (to create).
> [!WARNING]
> Three pairs are easy to blur. Factors that *shape* opportunities (geography, culture, technology, social change) explain why an opportunity exists, while factors that *trigger* recognition (a problem, feedback, disappointment, technology, experience, serendipity) explain why one person notices it. Session 4's idea versus business concept cut is about how developed the concept is, while Session 1's idea versus opportunity cut is about sustainable customer value. And "flash of insight" does not contradict "entrepreneurship is learnable": Jobs's own quote frames insight as connecting prior experience, which is why the deck pairs it with a journal and a network habit.
> [!SUMMARY]
> Week 3 turns "where do ideas come from, and is this one any good" into three practiceable habits plus a worksheet, and the book adds the cheap experiment that comes next.
