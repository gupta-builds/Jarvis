## Key ideas (short)
- A **business plan** is "a written representation of where a company is going, how it will get there, and what it will look like once it arrives," and its value is in the **process**: it begins when you start thinking about the venture and stays a living document.
- The plan is a **story**: a tagline gives it a unifying plot line, and the general format is kept because stakeholders read for it (the book says venture capitalists may scan a plan for as little as five minutes, p. 222).
- A **concept statement** is four things (name, product or service, target customers, value proposition), and a **value proposition** answers "why should customers choose you over others?"
- Two lists of **target-customer mistakes** (too broad, no research, ignoring feedback, personal preference, underestimating competition; and willingness to pay, a non-profitable segment, psychographics, failing to evolve, awareness mistaken for interest) pair with ten **willingness-to-pay tests**, from surveys to conversion from free to paid.
- The **executive summary** is the book's "book jacket" section (p. 223) and is drafted after the full plan (p. 224), yet the class practices on one (UpDown Express) first.
- The course gives its own **venture formation status report** template (an eight-slide structure from Canvas), which differs from the book's 10 to 12 slide pitch deck.

### 1. Agenda and framing (Sessions 8 and 9, Mon 10/5 and Wed 10/7)
Slide 2 lists the agenda as the business plan (why, what, how), Quiz 1, group formation, and Chapter 8. The first slide's speaker note says the venture formation status report is meant to help you start early on a written plan, giving the team time to iterate.
### 2. What a business plan is and why you need one (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slide 3 defines it as "a written representation of where a company is going, how it will get there, and what it will look like once it arrives." Slide 4 gives four reasons: it makes you think critically about every aspect of the venture, it serves as a benchmark to monitor progress, it describes the details of how you will exploit your opportunity, and it creates occasions to raise critical questions and to research and find answers.
### 3. Business planning as an ongoing process (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slide 5: planning begins when you start thinking about a new venture, you accumulate a critical mass of learning, you organize summary information to share your idea and to identify gaps, you focus on the critical aspects of the business model, and the plan is a "living document" that keeps evolving.
### 4. Your business plan as a story; taglines (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slide 6: the key takeaways for your business are found in the process of developing a well-written story. Ask how stakeholders will interpret your plan, stick to the general format of business plans, and use a tagline to create a unifying plot line that organizes your thinking. Slide 7 lists the example taglines under Examples above.
### 5. Concept statement outline (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slide 8: for one of your business ideas, give it a temporary name, describe the product or service being offered, identify your target customers, and state your value proposition.
### 6. Mistakes in judging target customers (Sessions 8 and 9)
Two slides, 9 and 11. First list: targeting too broad an audience, lack of customer research, ignoring customer feedback, assuming personal preferences reflect market needs, underestimating market competition. Second list, titled mistakes startups make: misjudging customer willingness to pay, targeting a non-profitable segment, overlooking psychographic data, failure to evolve with the market, assuming awareness equals interest.
### 7. Value proposition (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slides 10, 13, and 15 repeat one definition: a clear statement that explains how a product or service solves a specific problem, delivers unique benefits, and provides value to customers, distinguishing it from competitors. It answers "Why should customers choose your product or service over others?" and "What unique value does it offer?" Slides 16 to 19 give the Zoom, Airbnb, Dropbox, and Stripe examples. Slide 20 lists ten qualities of an effective value proposition: clarity, specificity, unique differentiation, customer focus, tangible benefits, emotional appeal, credibility, conciseness, call to action, consistency.
### 8. Testing willingness to pay (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slides 12 and 14, interleaved with the value-proposition slides. First list: conduct surveys and interviews, use landing pages with pricing, offer pre-orders or crowdfunding, run small-scale paid experiments, analyze competitor pricing. Second list: test with a minimum viable product, monitor engagement with pricing discussions, offer free trials with paid upgrade options, assess willingness to pay during sales pitches, track conversion rates from free to paid users.
### 9. Elements of a business plan and the executive summary exercise (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slide 22 lists the elements: executive summary, table of contents, the opportunity (industry and market analysis), marketing mix, product or service description and operational plan, management and organization, financial plan, funding request, appendices and exhibits. Slide 21 is a bare "Example" divider. Slides 23 to 33 work one executive summary, UpDown Express. Slide 24 asks the class what is executed well in each section, what can be improved, and to be prepared to share. Slide 25 adds "Looking ahead: writing a mini business plan." Slide 33 annotates the summary with prompts, as printed: what value does this segment bring to the summary, what might be more important to convey to VCs; what is the immediate next phase and what resources are needed to implement it; the mission of the venture and how to accomplish it; highlights the value proposition; highlights the industry landscape and competitive advantage over rivals and substitutes; founding team and ownership structure information; a sharp, direct, focused description of the business.
### 10. Improving your presentation (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slides 34 to 36. Be natural, be conversational, make seamless transitions between group members, finish on time. Improve eye contact, tell us what the product is or what your company does in one sentence at the beginning, walk us through how it works, think more about competition (direct and substitutes), and be clear about who the target market is.
### 11. Team member evaluation (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slide 37 asks how your teammates will evaluate your contributions. The deck states that you must make a positive contribution and that if you shirk, the group will kick you out or give you a low or even zero grade.
### 12. Slides for your presentation: the venture formation status report (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slide 38, sourced to the Venture Formation Status Report on Canvas. Slide 1: a one-slide executive summary covering accomplishments, challenges, and proposed solutions. Slide 2: industry and external (PEST) analysis, with Porter's Five Forces, the strategic decisions made from them, and data where possible. Slide 3: product or service design (diagrams, flowchart or blueprint, with a basic bill of materials in the appendix), outsourcing considerations, and safety, regulation, and insurance; cost estimates for a contract manufacturer are required for the written plan only. Slide 4: marketing and promotional plan, with initial ideas and survey or interview questions. Slides 5 to 7: ownership and organizational structure, with a team skills-gap analysis, the team's thoughts on the ownership and control split and decision rules, customized bios, and roles that map to an organization chart. Slide 8: works cited in APA, MLA, or Chicago style. Appendices: a signed team contract, the bill of materials, and contract-manufacturer cost estimates.
### 13. Feasibility analysis, marketing, and founding team prompts (Sessions 8 and 9)
Slide 39 lists feasibility questions for the industry and market (use Porter's Five Forces and PEST; demographics, trends, and life-cycle stage; barriers to entry; technology and R&D; typical profit margins; opinion leaders; what distributors, competitors, and retailers say; the target market's demographics; the first customer's profile; how you approached customers; who your competitors are) and for the product or service (features and benefits, development tasks, timeline and cost, window of opportunity, intellectual property, outsourcing, differentiation). Slide 40 asks, for marketing, "recall the 4 P's" and how you will create awareness, build understanding of your brand, reach target customers, and produce sales quickly; and, for the founding team, what experience the team has, what the gaps are and how to fill them, and the rationale for the organization structure, ownership split, and decision-making process.

### What the book adds beyond the deck
The book supplies the evidence for "planning matters": Liao and Gartner found entrepreneurs who complete a business plan are 2.6 times more likely to launch (p. 220), while an Inc. survey found 65% of fast-growing firms ran a business model that differed significantly from their written plan (p. 219), and Eisenhower's line is the reconciliation: "plans are useless, but planning is indispensable" (p. 219). It also describes the stages the deck skips: informal testing with co-founders, family, and advisors, then a short initial concept summary of under five pages (p. 220-221). The executive summary has eight named subsections (opportunity, business concept, industry overview, target market, competitive advantage, business model and economics, team, financial snapshot) and is written after the full plan (p. 224). Book-only tools: the **Competitive Profile Matrix** (a 1 to 5 rating against rivals on key success factors, p. 226-228), the **operating cycle** (p. 233-234), the **Gantt chart** (p. 235-236), the monthly **burn rate** (p. 237-239), and the Offering section's capital required, valuation, equity offered, and use of proceeds (p. 239). The Gravyty case (p. 243-274, 294-301) shows a bottom-up financial model built from IRS Form 990 filings and a requirement that the first non-network "stranger" pay full price, which is the deck's pre-order and "assess willingness to pay in sales pitches" tests executed.

The deck's executive summary maps to book section 2, table of contents to 3, "the opportunity" to 4 (industry, customer, and competitor analysis), marketing mix to 6 (seven dimensions), product or service description and operational plan to 5, 7, and 8 (company and product, operations, development), management and organization to 9 (team), financial plan to 12, funding request to 11 (offering), and appendices to 13. The book's cover (section 1) and **Critical Risks** (section 10) have no counterpart among the deck's nine elements. This mapping is my reading of the two lists, not a statement in either source.
### Plan types and the slide-count conflict
The book distinguishes an operational plan (over 80 pages, internal), an investor business plan (25 to 40 pages, with 25-page plans funded more often than 40-page ones), and a summary plan or pitch deck of about 10 pages or 10 to 12 slides (p. 240-241). This course's own requirements differ: the status report is an eight-slide structure, and the Session 7 idea pitch allowed at most five slides (see [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Weekly/Week - 4|Week - 4]]). ## Lecture-to-textbook synthesis
==A business plan is a research-and-story process rather than a document: the concept statement, value proposition, and willingness-to-pay tests are the early questions that make the later thirteen-section plan credible to a reader who spot-reads it.==
*Mechanism:* The deck says planning begins when you start thinking and the plan stays a living document (slide 5). The book gives the sequence behind that: informal tests with co-founders and advisors, a concept summary under five pages, then the full plan (p. 220-221). Eisenhower's "plans are useless, but planning is indispensable" explains why 65% of fast-growing firms depart from their written plan (p. 219) and yet planners launch 2.6 times more often (p. 220): the value was in the learning, not the artifact. The Timmons Framework from Chapter 2 is the reason the plan has to align Opportunity, Team, and Resources at once (p. 218, 235).
- Lecture example/scenario: the UpDown Express executive summary exercise asks what is executed well in each section and what a VC would still need. The summary opens with the service, route, hours, and price, then covers competitors, ownership (five 20% shares), and the funding plan.
- Textbook connection: [[20_Progress/Degree/MGMT 3015/Textbook/Chapter - 8|Chapter - 8]]. Gravyty's two-page executive summary and 11-slide deck (p. 244-274, 300-301) are the book's executed version of the same artifacts, and its first-stranger-pays-full-price test is the deck's willingness-to-pay list in practice (p. 294).
- Concept links: [[Concept - Value Proposition]] (to create), [[Concept - Business Plan Story Model]] (to create), [[Concept - Competitive Profile Matrix]] (to create).
> [!WARNING]
> Do not read the executive summary as an introduction. The book says it must hook the reader with market size, growth, and competitive advantage rather than registration details (p. 223-224), and that it is drafted last (p. 224). Do not read plan length as quality either: the 25-page plans funded more often than 40-page ones because investors spot-read (p. 240).
