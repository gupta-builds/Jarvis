Draft to copy by hand:

Pico finally tests the thread I've been pulling since Journal 1. Brennan's opinion turns on motive: a board can remove a book for vulgarity, not to suppress "the ideas or social perspectives discussed in them." Blackmun narrows that further: removal is only unconstitutional if the purpose is disapproving the idea itself. Minnesota's Subdivision 1 sets a lower bar than either: removal "based solely on" viewpoint is banned outright, no motive test required.

Melissa is the cleanest case yet. Huck Finn had a slur to isolate; Camp of the Saints had a whole argument to indict. Melissa has neither. A fourth grader wants to play Charlotte in the school play and her teacher won't let her audition, because the roster lists her as a boy. There's no passage to flag for vulgarity, because there isn't one. Every objection to this book is an objection to the character existing, which is precisely the "narrowly partisan" suppression Brennan warned about and precisely what Subdivision 1 forbids.

So it's the most challenged of the three. The law gets more protective exactly where the appetite to ban gets strongest. That doesn't feel like a coincidence to me anymore.

(~191 words.)

### Week 1 - legal foundations (9/8, 9/10)
**United States v. One Book Called "Ulysses,"** 5 F. Supp. 182 (S.D.N.Y. 1933) - found only as a citation, not a local file; full opinion cross-checked by Journal 1 against [law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/5/182/2250768/). Judge Woolsey's holding: the test isn't whether an isolated passage could corrupt the most susceptible reader (the English *Hicklin* rule), it's whether the book taken as a whole, read by a person with average sex instincts, has the effect of stirring the sex impulses or leading to sexually impure thoughts - and whether *Ulysses* was written with pornographic intent or with "a sincerity and honesty of purpose." Captured in `ENGL 1004/Lecture/9_10_26.pdf`.

**Minnesota Session Laws 2024, Chapter 109, Art. 7, Sec. 2 (codified as Minn. Stat. 134.51)** - read in full from `ENGL 1004/Readings/Minnesota Law.pdf`. Full subdivision breakdown:
- Subd. 1 - **Book banning prohibited.** A public library "must not ban, remove, or otherwise restrict access to a book or other material based solely on its viewpoint or the messages, ideas, or opinions it conveys." (The clause Journals 1 and 2 both leaned on.)
- Subd. 2 - Definitions: "public library" covers city/county libraries, joint city-school libraries, school/charter library media centers, and public higher-ed libraries.
- Subd. 3 - **Limitations** (the carve-out Journal 1 cited): nothing in the section stops a library from declining/removing a book for (1) practical reasons - shelf space, damage, obsolescence; (2) "legitimate pedagogical concerns, including but not limited to the appropriateness of potentially sensitive topics for the library's intended audience ... or the likelihood of causing a material and substantial disruption"; or (3) compliance with other state/federal law. Subd. 3(b) also preserves parent/guardian rights under Minn. Stat. 120B.20.
- Subd. 4 - Collection management: a library can't discipline or discriminate against an employee for complying with this section.
- Subd. 5 - **Library materials policy**: every governing body must adopt a written selection/challenge/reconsideration policy, administered only by a licensed library media specialist or someone with a library-science credential, and must report every completed challenge to the Commissioner of Education (title/author, hearing date and minutes, outcome, contact person).
- **Minn. Stat. 120B.20 (Parental Curriculum Review)**, included on the same PDF: every district must let a parent/guardian/adult student review instructional materials and arrange alternative instruction if they object - the district isn't required to pay for it, and a student can't be penalized academically just for using it.

This file is a four-statute *summary*, not the verbatim code text, so any claim about exact phrase-for-phrase overlap with Minnesota's language (Journal 1 called the two "nearly identical") can't be fully confirmed from this file alone - flagging that as unverified, not wrong.
- Fla. Stat. § 1006.28 - school boards must adopt library media center selection/removal procedures, publish reading lists online, and give parents/county residents a formal objection process.
- Fla. Stat. § 1006.31 - substantive standard for state-adopted instructional materials: accurate, objective, balanced, noninflammatory, free of pornography/§847.012 material, age-appropriate.

- Fla. Stat. § 1001.42 - the "Parental Rights in Education" provisions restricting classroom instruction on sexual orientation/gender identity, with parental-notification and objection-resolution rules.

**Island Trees Union Free School District v. Pico (1982)** - `ENGL 1004/Lecture/9_22-24.pdf`, read in full. Splintered 5-4 ruling, 7 of 9 justices wrote separately.
- Facts: the school board ordered certain books removed as "anti-American, anti-Christian, anti-Semitic, and just plain filthy." Trial court granted summary judgment for the board; the court of appeals reversed, finding a genuine factual dispute over whether the board acted to remove vulgarity (permissible) or to suppress ideas (impermissible).
- Question presented: does removing books from junior high/high school libraries based on content violate the First Amendment?
- Brennan and Marshall (affirm): the "special characteristics of the school library" mean removal implicates students' First Amendment rights; officials may not remove books on "narrowly partisan or political" grounds - that would be "official suppression of ideas."
- Blackmun (affirm, narrower): rejected treating the library as distinct from the school and rejected any student "right to receive" in a public school context, but agreed removal is unconstitutional when its *purpose* is restricting access to political ideas or social perspectives out of simple disapproval.
- White (affirm the judgment only): expressly declined to reach the First Amendment question at all, preferring to let the factual dispute get resolved at trial first.
- Dissent: removal implicates no First Amendment right because (1) the books remained available elsewhere, (2) no "right to receive information" exists in a school setting, (3) courts should defer educational policy to locally accountable officials.
